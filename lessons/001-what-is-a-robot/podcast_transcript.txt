Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 1: What Is a Robot? Welcome to the beginning of our robotics course. You and RoboRover are starting with a question that sounds simple, but matters throughout engineering: what counts as a robot? Before circuits, movement code, or sensors, we need a clear mental model. A robot is not defined by appearance. It may use wheels, arms, tracks, legs, or another mechanism. It may be controlled directly by a person, follow a stored program, or choose actions using software. For this course, we will use this working description: a robot is a physical machine commonly treated as a robot in engineering practice, whose controlled actuators perform a physical task. Its immediate actions may be selected by a human operator, a preprogrammed controller, or autonomous software. This is a teaching description, not a universal necessary-and-sufficient test. Engineering communities use the word robot differently in different contexts, so not every boundary case has one universally correct yes-or-no answer. There are three ideas to keep separate. First is robot identity: what kind of physical machine it is. Second is decision authority: who or what selects its immediate actions. Third is feedback: whether measured information affects current or future actions. Start with a bicycle. It is a physical machine that performs a task, carrying a person, but the rider supplies the immediate decisions and physical effort. Now imagine a motorized rover. Its wheels and motors are actuators, meaning components that produce controlled physical action. A human might drive it with a joystick. A stored program might tell it to move for a particular time. Or software might use sensor readings to decide what to do next. The wheels and motors do not decide by themselves. They carry out decisions made elsewhere in the system. Robot identity is separate from autonomy. Suppose RoboRover has wheels, motors, a battery, and a radio receiver. If a student drives it with a joystick, it is a teleoperated robot. Teleoperated means operated from a distance by a human. If it follows a stored movement sequence, it is still a robot, but its immediate actions are selected by a preprogrammed controller. If it measures its surroundings and selects task-level actions using software, it is an autonomous robot. The hardware can stay the same while the decision authority changes. A robot operates in an environment: the relevant physical surroundings that can affect its task or be affected by it. For RoboRover, that might include the floor, a box it may approach or push, walls, people, and lighting that affects a camera sensor. What counts as relevant depends on the question. For a wheel-motion experiment, the floor may matter greatly while room lighting may not. Now consider feedback. Feedback control broadly means that a measured state or output influences a current or future control action in relation to a desired behavior or state. A thermostat is a useful example. Its desired state might be a selected temperature of 21 degrees Celsius. It measures the room temperature, then turns heating on or off. A thermostat is not normally called a robot, but it does use feedback. Also, preprogrammed and feedback-controlled are not opposites. An industrial robot arm can follow a stored task sequence while its local controllers use measured joint positions to adjust motor commands. Here is a quick pause and predict. A rover has the same hardware in three modes: human-directed, preprogrammed, and autonomous. Which mode must be considered a robot? Take a moment. The answer is all three, under our course description. The difference is who or what selects the immediate action, not whether the physical machine suddenly becomes a robot. Let’s work through a movement example. The simple distance model is distance equals average speed multiplied by elapsed time. Suppose RoboRover moves at an average speed of zero point two five metres per second for eight seconds. The calculation is zero point two five multiplied by eight, which equals two point zero zero metres. So, under this simple model, RoboRover travels two metres during the command. Now imagine the physical task is moving a lightweight blue block toward a collection tray. In a teleoperated version, a human watches and decides when to move and stop. In a preprogrammed version, the controller commands forward motion for eight seconds. In an autonomous version, software uses sensor measurements and chooses when to move or stop. All three versions can be robots. Again, the changing feature is decision authority. The most important engineering caveat is that commanded speed is not automatically measured speed. A motor command such as fifty percent power does not guarantee a particular velocity. Wheels can rotate at different rates. One wheel may slip on dust or a smooth surface. Battery voltage can change, and starting or stopping may take time. The rover may also veer from a straight path. That means the two-metre result is a model-based prediction, not a guarantee. A careful engineer measures the actual distance, repeats the test, and compares the physical result with the ideal calculation. This is an early example of the difference between a model and reality. The Python lab explores this distinction conceptually. It simulates the same RoboRover hardware on a one-dimensional track, with one-second time steps and an average forward speed of zero point five metres per second. One run uses commands chosen by a human. Another follows a stored sequence. The autonomous run checks whether an obstacle is detected and selects stop commands when it is. The lab verifies that the autonomous commands are forward, forward, stop, stop. The human-directed and autonomous runs each finish at one metre, while the preprogrammed run finishes at one point five metres because it contains three forward commands. The plotted position traces rise during forward motion and remain level during stops. The practical takeaway is to ask two separate questions whenever you examine a system. Is this physical system commonly treated as a robot? And who or what selects its immediate actions?
