Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 1, “What Is a Robot?” We’re beginning with a question that sounds simple but has several careful answers: what makes a machine a robot? Throughout this course, we’ll follow a fictional machine called RoboRover. It’s a small wheeled machine with two powered wheels, a battery, a simple computer, sensors that measure parts of its surroundings, and controlled actuators, such as motors, that create motion. Today, we are not designing a navigation algorithm or a feedback controller. Instead, we’re learning how to describe RoboRover accurately. Here is the course’s working description: a robot is a physical machine commonly treated as a robot in engineering practice, whose controlled actuators perform a physical task. Its immediate actions may be selected by a human operator, a preprogrammed controller, or autonomous software. This is a teaching description, not a universal necessary-and-sufficient test. In other words, there is no single boundary that settles every borderline case. Engineers, manufacturers, researchers, and ordinary users may use the word robot somewhat differently. The most important idea is this: robot identity and decision authority are separate questions. Imagine three identical RoboRovers. Rover A is driven by a person using a joystick. Rover B repeats a stored sequence: forward, forward, turn, stop. Rover C chooses its next task-level action using software. The hardware is the same in all three cases. What changes is who or what selects the immediate actions. A teleoperated rover can still be a robot even though a human chooses its immediate movement. An industrial robot arm can be a robot even when it follows a preprogrammed sequence. And an autonomous vacuum can be a robot because software selects task-level actions. It also helps to distinguish the machine from its environment. The rover’s wheels, motors, battery, computer, sensors, and chassis are part of the machine. The floor, walls, objects, light, and people around it are parts of the environment. A camera mounted on RoboRover is part of the robot. The box it observes is normally part of the environment, unless we deliberately choose a larger system boundary. When you meet an unfamiliar machine, ask three questions. What physical system is the machine? Who or what selects its immediate action? And does measured state influence a current or future control action? That last question introduces feedback. Feedback means that a measured state or output influences a current or future control action in relation to a desired behavior or state. A simple way to say it is: measure, compare with the desired state, act, and measure again. Consider a thermostat. Someone sets a desired temperature, such as 20 degrees Celsius. The thermostat measures the room temperature. If the temperature is below the heating threshold, it turns heating on. When the temperature reaches the relevant threshold, it turns heating off. The rule is predetermined, but the action responds to a measurement. That is feedback control. Feedback does not have to be continuous or proportional. A threshold rule, including one with hysteresis, can still be feedback. Now, pause and predict: a sensor detects an object once, starts a fixed ten-second motor sequence, and the later actions never use the measured distance. Would we call that sustained feedback regulation? Brief beat. No. Under this course’s convention, the sensor started the sequence, but the measured state was not repeatedly used to adjust later actions. Task-level autonomy and feedback are independent. Software can select task-level actions without demonstrating feedback, and a non-autonomous device can use feedback to regulate a physical variable. Let’s work through one RoboRover example. Suppose the rover is asked to deliver a lightweight sensor package to a marked location 2.4 meters away. Its motor controller is set for an ideal forward speed of 0.60 meters per second, and its stored task says: drive forward for 4.0 seconds, then stop. For constant speed, ideal distance equals speed multiplied by time. So, 0.60 meters per second multiplied by 4.0 seconds gives 2.4 meters. The seconds cancel, leaving meters. The rover’s wheel motors are the controlled actuators performing the physical task, and the stored controller selects the immediate action. So this is a robot performing a preprogrammed task. But here is the most important engineering caveat: 2.4 meters is an ideal prediction, not a guarantee. The battery voltage may change. The wheels may slip. The motor speed may not match its setting exactly. There may be a command delay, unequal torque between the wheels, or an uneven floor. If the rover stops at 2.2 meters, the equation has not become useless. It has shown that one or more assumptions were inaccurate. Engineering means comparing a model with a measurement and investigating the difference. Calibration is especially important: it relates a sensor measurement or actuator command to a physical quantity. If the controller assumes 0.60 meters per second but the actual speed is lower because of a weak battery or carpet friction, the distance prediction will be wrong. The Python lab explores this same distinction conceptually. It models the same RoboRover hardware on a one-dimensional track. In human mode, a person enters each immediate command. In programmed mode, a stored list of commands runs. In autonomous mode, simple software checks an idealized simulated position and selects the next command. The physical model stays the same while decision authority changes. The lab is intentionally simple. It does not demonstrate perception, navigation, planning, or meaningful adaptation to an uncertain environment. The autonomous example uses an internal simulated position variable, not a simulated sensor measurement. Its purpose is to make the relationship between hardware and decision authority visible. For a memorable comparison, in programmed and autonomous modes, five forward commands move the rover five steps of one meter each, reaching 5.0 meters. In human mode, if the commands are forward, forward, stop, forward, stop, there are three forward steps, so the rover ends at 3.0 meters. The person selected the immediate actions, but the physical machine is still commonly treated as a robot. Keep a few common mistakes in mind.
