Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 6, Electricity for Roboticists. Last class, RoboRover described motion using distance and speed. But motion also requires energy: the capacity to cause change or perform work. Power is the rate at which energy is transferred. Energy is measured in joules or watt-hours, while power is measured in watts. RoboRover moves because a circuit transfers electrical energy from a source, such as a battery, to a motor, where that energy becomes mechanical motion. Today, we focus on three ideas that help us understand that circuit: voltage, current, and resistance. Voltage is electrical potential difference between two points. It is measured in volts. Voltage can drive charge through a circuit. Current is the rate at which electric charge flows past a point. It is measured in amperes, or amps. One ampere is one coulomb of charge per second. Resistance describes how strongly a component opposes current. It is measured in ohms. A useful, limited analogy is to imagine water moving through pipes. Voltage is like a pressure difference encouraging flow. Current is like the amount of water passing a point each second. Resistance is like a narrow or rough section of pipe that makes flow harder. This analogy builds intuition, but it is not a complete physical model. Charge is not consumed by a load. A circuit transfers energy while charge continues around the conducting path. Current is a circuit variable, and charge motion in a conductor is not identical to bulk water circulation. For RoboRover, the battery provides a voltage difference, wires provide conducting paths, a motor driver controls power delivered to the motor, a resistor can limit current, and a switch or transistor can open, close, or regulate part of a circuit. A circuit needs a complete conducting path. Conventional current leaves the source’s positive terminal, passes through the load and active circuit elements, and returns to the source’s negative terminal. If that path is broken, continuous current cannot flow through the intended loop. Pause and predict. If RoboRover’s battery is connected to only one motor terminal, while the other motor terminal is disconnected, will the motor turn continuously? Brief beat. No. The intended path is open, so continuous current cannot pass through the motor. Now consider a motor driver using an H-bridge. The battery’s positive connection goes to the driver’s motor-supply input, labeled V-M. The battery’s negative connection goes to the specified power return, commonly labeled ground, or G-N-D. The motor connects between two switched output nodes, often called O-U-T-1 and O-U-T-2. It does not connect between one motor output and some unspecified point. An H-bridge uses switches to control the direction and power delivered to the motor. In one forward-current state, current can flow from V-M through an upper switch, out through O-U-T-1, through the motor, back through O-U-T-2, through a lower switch, and then to G-N-D and the battery negative terminal. This is a simplified teaching model. Exact pin names, capacitors, enable pins, current limits, and wiring requirements depend on the driver datasheet. Connect logic ground only as specified by that datasheet. A shared power and logic ground is common, but it is not a universal default. The bridge must also prevent shoot-through. That occurs when both the high-side and low-side switches in the same leg turn on together, creating a near-short from V-M to ground. Real drivers may use interlock logic, dead time, current limiting, or other protection. Follow the driver’s control requirements rather than manually operating bridge switches. Voltage is measured between points, not as an isolated property of one point. A battery labeled 7.4 volts has a nominal voltage of approximately 7.4 volts under specified conditions. Its actual voltage can differ depending on whether it is measured with no significant load or while current is being drawn. Battery internal resistance, wires, connectors, protection circuits, and battery state can cause the voltage to fall under load. This reduction is called voltage sag. Every connected component must be suitable for the applied voltage. For an approximately ohmic resistor, Ohm’s law says voltage equals current times resistance. In plain language, current equals voltage divided by resistance, and resistance equals voltage divided by current. Keep the units attached: volts, amps, and ohms. Electrical power is the rate of electrical energy transfer. Power equals voltage times current. For a resistor, you can also use current squared times resistance, or voltage squared divided by resistance. Compare calculated power with the component’s rated power, and leave engineering margin. Here is a memorable robotics example. Suppose RoboRover has a five-volt indicator branch with a red LED and a 220-ohm series resistor. Assume the LED’s forward voltage is two volts. The resistor receives the remaining three volts. Current is three volts divided by 220 ohms, which is approximately 0.0136 amps, or 13.6 milliamps. The resistor’s power is approximately 0.0409 watts. The LED is not an ohmic resistor, so the two-volt forward-drop value is an assumption that depends on current and temperature. Check the LED’s maximum current, the resistor’s power rating, supply tolerance, and thermal conditions. If the resistor were only 100 ohms, the current would rise to 30 milliamps, which could exceed the LED’s safe operating range. At fixed voltage, increasing resistance decreases current. It also decreases resistor power, because power equals voltage squared divided by resistance. In series circuits, resistances add and the same current flows through each component. In parallel circuits, branches share voltage, their currents add at the source, and a parallel branch can reduce total resistance and increase source current. The Python lab models a resistor-only branch. Conceptually, it calculates current and resistor power for several resistance values, checks expected numerical results with assertions, rejects invalid inputs such as negative voltage or nonpositive resistance, and lets you change a classroom comparison threshold. It does not simulate LED nonlinearity, motor startup current, battery sag, or motor-driver switching. The most important engineering caveat is that real robot hardware is not an ideal equation. A motor is not a fixed resistor.
