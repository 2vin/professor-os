This is Class 7, “Motors: Turning Electricity into Motion.” In Class 6, we learned how voltage, current, resistance, power, batteries, switches, and electrical connections allow a robot to receive electrical energy. Now RoboRover needs to do something with that energy. A battery does not make RoboRover move by itself. It provides electrical energy to a motor, and the motor changes that energy into rotation. Wheels, gears, belts, fans, pumps, and robotic joints can then use that rotation to perform physical work. Today, we will focus on two important mechanical quantities: torque and rotational speed. We will also connect motor-shaft torque to wheel torque, wheel force, and the rover’s forward speed. First, a quick electrical connection. Voltage is the electrical potential difference that can push charge through a circuit. Current is the rate at which charge flows. Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer, calculated as voltage multiplied by current. A motor uses that electrical power, but not all of it becomes useful motion. Some becomes heat, sound, friction, and vibration. Pause and predict: if RoboRover’s wheels move from a smooth floor onto carpet, will the motors usually spin faster, slower, or at the same speed? Give yourself a moment. The usual answer is slower. Carpet generally creates more resistance, increasing the mechanical load on the motors. A real motor’s speed commonly decreases as its load torque increases. The big idea is that torque and speed are different things. Torque is the turning effect, or twisting strength, around an axis. Its unit is the newton-metre. You can picture torque with a screwdriver: pushing harder, or applying the same force farther from the turning point, creates more turning effect. The basic relationship is torque equals force multiplied by distance from the axis. For example, a ten-newton force applied 0.2 metres from a hinge produces 2 newton-metres of torque. For a wheel, wheel torque becomes an ideal tangential force at the wheel’s edge. That force equals wheel torque divided by wheel radius. A smaller wheel produces more ideal pushing force for the same torque, but it travels a shorter distance per revolution. RPM means revolutions per minute. A speed of 120 RPM means the shaft completes 120 full turns in one minute. RPM is not the same as RoboRover’s forward speed. To calculate forward speed, we also need the wheel radius. One revolution moves the wheel through one circumference. Circumference equals two times pi times the radius. If the wheel rotates at a certain number of revolutions per minute, its ideal rolling speed is the number of revolutions multiplied by circumference, divided by 60 to convert minutes into seconds. This assumes rolling without slipping and does not include losses or tire deformation. Now let’s see how gearing changes the picture. A gearbox trades speed for torque. If the gearbox ratio is 20 to 1, the output turns about 20 times slower than the motor input. With an efficiency of 80 percent, a simple estimate says wheel speed equals motor speed divided by 20, while wheel torque equals 80 percent multiplied by 20 multiplied by motor torque. Here is a memorable example. Suppose a motor produces 0.10 newton-metres at 3,000 RPM. With a 20-to-1 gearbox operating at 80 percent efficiency, the wheel speed is approximately 150 RPM. The wheel torque is approximately 1.6 newton-metres. If the wheel radius is 0.04 metres, the ideal wheel-edge force is 1.6 divided by 0.04, or 40 newtons. That 40-newton result is an ideal wheel-edge force, not a guarantee of 40 newtons pushing RoboRover across the floor. The tire may slip. Static friction, tire deformation, uneven loading, battery voltage, driver current limits, and mechanical losses can all reduce the usable force. This is the most important engineering caveat: calculated force and speed are predictions to test, not promises. The quantities must also be defined correctly. Motor-shaft torque is not automatically wheel torque. A gearbox must be included, along with its efficiency, before calculating wheel force. Motors also have a torque-speed trade-off. In a simplified DC motor model, the motor spins near its highest speed with little mechanical load. As load torque increases, speed decreases. At stall, the shaft is not turning, but the motor may produce its highest starting torque. A straight torque-speed line is useful for teaching, but it is only an approximate steady-state relationship for a specified supply voltage and approximate temperature. It does not fully describe startup, acceleration, sudden load changes, heating, or continuous operation. Most importantly, a stalled motor can draw high current and heat rapidly. Running one stalled for too long can damage the motor, driver, wiring, or battery system. Let’s work through another short calculation. Suppose RoboRover has two identical drive motors. Each provides 0.18 newton-metres of torque, each directly drives one wheel, and each wheel has a radius of 0.035 metres. The ideal force from one wheel is 0.18 divided by 0.035, or about 5.14 newtons. If both wheels have adequate traction and operate comparably, the ideal total force is about 10.29 newtons. The wheel circumference is two times pi times 0.035, or approximately 0.220 metres. At 120 RPM, the ideal rolling speed is 120 multiplied by 0.220, divided by 60, which is about 0.440 metres per second, or 44 centimetres per second. Notice that force and speed are separate operating quantities. The motor’s available torque usually changes with speed, and gearing changes both torque and speed.
