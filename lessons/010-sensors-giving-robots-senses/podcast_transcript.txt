Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 10: Sensors: Giving Robots Senses. RoboRover can now use gears to trade speed for strength. A gear train can help its wheels push harder or turn more slowly. But gears do not tell RoboRover what is happening around it. That is the job of sensors. A sensor measures something about the robot or its environment and produces a usable signal. A distance sensor measures distance. A wheel encoder measures rotation. A temperature sensor measures temperature. A camera measures patterns of light. Today, we will focus on three foundations: measurement, noise, and calibration. The central idea is simple: a sensor does not transmit perfect truth. It converts a physical condition into an electrical or digital signal, and software interprets that signal as an estimate. Imagine RoboRover facing a wall and asking, “How far away is it?” The physical distance might be one meter. The sensor may produce a voltage, a pulse timing, a digital count, or an image. Electronics report a value, and software converts that value into an estimated distance. A useful model is: reading equals true value plus bias plus noise. The reading is what the sensor reports. The true value is what we want to know. Bias is a repeatable error, such as always reading five centimeters too high. Noise is variation that changes from one reading to another. This distinction matters. A measurement is a number connected to a physical quantity and a unit. Saying that a sensor read “50” is incomplete. Fifty what? Centimeters? Millimeters? Something else? We also need to consider the sensor’s range, uncertainty, and operating conditions. Noise is unwanted variation. It can come from electrical interference, vibration from motors and gears, changing lighting, a rough or angled target, small timing differences, air movement, or temperature changes. One common response is to take several readings and calculate their average. Add the readings together, then divide by the number of readings. Averaging can reduce the effect of changing noise. But it does not remove constant bias. If every reading is too high by five centimeters, the average will also tend to be too high by five centimeters. Here is a memorable example. Suppose RoboRover is placed exactly half a meter from a flat test board. A ruler establishes that known reference distance. The sensor reports five readings: zero point five six, zero point five four, zero point five five, zero point five seven, and zero point five three meters. Their average is zero point five five meters. Since the known distance is zero point five zero meters, the estimated bias is positive zero point zero five meters. In other words, this sensor tends to read five centimeters too large under these conditions. Now RoboRover moves to another location. The sensor reports one point zero eight, one point zero four, one point zero six, one point zero seven, and one point zero five meters. The raw average is one point zero six meters. To apply the calibration, subtract the estimated bias of zero point zero five meters. The corrected average becomes one point zero one meters. The correction improves the estimate, but the individual readings still vary. Calibration shifts the readings downward; it does not make every reading exactly one meter. Pause and predict: if the sensor has a positive bias and we subtract that bias, will the corrected readings be larger or smaller than the raw readings? And will they all become exactly one point zero zero meters? Brief beat. The corrected readings will be smaller, and they will not all become exactly one meter. Noise remains. Calibration removes an estimated repeatable offset, not every source of uncertainty. This brings us to accuracy and precision. Accuracy means closeness to the true or reference value. Precision means how tightly repeated readings agree with one another. A sensor can be precise but inaccurate. For example, it might repeatedly report one point zero six meters when the wall is actually one point zero zero meters away. The readings cluster closely, but they are shifted away from the truth. The most important engineering caveat is that a simple offset correction works best when the error is approximately constant. Real conditions can change. A calibration performed with RoboRover level on a cool, stationary workbench may become less accurate when the rover tilts, drives over a rough surface, vibrates, faces an angled target, or operates at a different temperature. Mounting also matters. If a distance sensor points slightly downward, it may measure the floor or a lower part of an object instead of the intended wall. Different sensor technologies may respond differently to shiny, soft, dark, or angled surfaces. Sensors also have minimum and maximum useful ranges, response delays, update-rate limits, and possible saturation, where the output reaches a limit. So calibration is an engineering process. Define the physical quantity and units. Use a known reference. Collect repeated readings. Calculate the error pattern. Test the correction under realistic conditions, and recalibrate if the setup changes. The Python lab simulates the calibration example conceptually. It stores reference and test readings, computes their averages, estimates the bias, subtracts that bias from later readings, and checks the arithmetic with assertions. It also plots raw and corrected readings against a one-meter comparison line. The expected result is that the corrected values appear lower than the raw values by the same amount, while still showing some variation. When experimenting, you can change the readings and predict the new averages before running the program. You can also calculate the range, which is the largest reading minus the smallest reading. Range gives a quick description of how far apart the readings are, although it is not a complete uncertainty analysis. Keep units explicit. One meter equals one hundred centimeters, so fifty centimeters equals zero point five zero meters. A program that expects meters must not silently receive centimeters. The practical takeaway is this: gears help RoboRover act, while sensors help it find out what happened.
