Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 12: Infrared Sensors. RoboRover has just learned to measure distance with an ultrasonic sensor. That sensor sends a sound pulse, waits for an echo, and estimates distance from the echo’s travel time. Today, RoboRover uses a different kind of invisible wave: infrared light, or IR. A reflective infrared proximity sensor sends out light, observes how much returns, and uses that returned light to notice nearby objects. The central difference is simple. An ultrasonic sensor asks, “How long did the echo take?” A reflective IR sensor asks, “How much light came back?” Pause and predict: if a black object and a white object are placed at exactly the same distance from a reflective IR sensor, will they necessarily produce the same reading? Brief beat. The answer is no. Dark surfaces often return less infrared light than pale, reflective surfaces, although the exact behavior depends on the material and on the sensor design. A reflective IR sensor is like a tiny flashlight and light detector mounted together. First, an IR emitter sends out infrared light. The light strikes a nearby surface. Some is absorbed, some is scattered, and some is reflected. Then an IR receiver measures part of the light that returns. Humans usually cannot see infrared light, but electronic receivers can detect it. As RoboRover moves closer to an object, the returned signal often becomes stronger. But the sensor does not directly “see distance.” It sees a light signal that tends to change with distance and with the object’s surface properties. That distinction is the most important engineering caveat in this class. A reflective IR sensor is usually a proximity detector, not a perfect distance ruler. Imagine RoboRover approaching a cardboard box. A pale, rough box may return more infrared light than a very dark box at the same distance. The sensor could interpret the pale box as being closer, even though both boxes are equally far away. Angle matters too. If the sensor faces the box directly, much of the reflected light may return toward the receiver. If the sensor points at a sharp angle, the light may reflect away, producing an unexpectedly weak reading even when the object is close. Texture, sunlight, and nearby lighting can also affect the measurement. This differs from ultrasonic sensing. Ultrasonic distance estimation mainly depends on travel time. Infrared reflection depends strongly on returned light strength. Sunlight contains infrared energy, so strong ambient light can add noise or overwhelm the receiver, especially outdoors. Do not confuse a reflective IR sensor with a break-beam sensor. In a reflective sensor, the emitter and receiver are on the same side, and the system detects light returning from an object. In a break-beam arrangement, the emitter is on one side and the receiver is on the other. An object is detected when it interrupts the beam. For a simple teaching model, we can represent the IR signal with this relationship: signal equals K times R divided by distance squared. K is a scale constant for the sensor and setup. R is a relative reflectivity value. Distance is measured in centimetres. This model gives useful intuition. A more reflective surface produces a stronger signal. A greater distance produces a weaker signal. If distance doubles, the modeled signal becomes one quarter as large. Here is a worked example. Suppose the scale constant K is 100, the relative reflectivity R is 0.80, and the object is 8 centimetres away. The signal is 100 times 0.80 divided by 8 squared. That is 80 divided by 64, or 1.25 sensor units. Now move the same object to 6 centimetres. The signal becomes 80 divided by 36, approximately 2.22 sensor units. Suppose RoboRover uses a threshold of 2.0 units to decide that an object is close. At 8 centimetres, the reading is 1.25, so the object is not classified as close. At 6 centimetres, the reading is about 2.22, so RoboRover enters its close-object behavior. This is a threshold decision: below 2.0, continue; at or above 2.0, slow or stop. A threshold can be very useful when the robot does not need an exact distance. But it must be tested on real materials. In a practical calibration example, a sensor facing pale cardboard might read about 0.8 units at 12 centimetres, 1.0 at 10 centimetres, 1.4 at 8 centimetres, 2.1 at 6 centimetres, and 3.0 at 4 centimetres. Choosing 2.0 as the threshold would make RoboRover enter slow mode at approximately 6 centimetres. That threshold is not a magical distance detector. It depends on the sensor, the wall material, the sensor angle, its mounting height, the lighting environment, the robot’s speed, and its stopping distance. If the wall changes from pale cardboard to black tape, the same threshold may be crossed much later, or not at all. Also remember that a trigger is not the same as continuous distance control. Stopping when one reading crosses a threshold is a sensor-based decision. Continuously adjusting speed to maintain a chosen gap is a more advanced control problem. In the Python lab, you will explore a simplified simulation rather than hardware control. The program models RoboRover beginning 12 centimetres from a pale object and moving one centimetre closer per step. It calculates the signal using the simplified equation and reports the first time the reading reaches the 2.0-unit threshold. With the original settings, the first close decision occurs at 6 centimetres, after six one-centimetre steps. You can change the reflectivity or the threshold and observe what happens. Reducing reflectivity from 0.80 to 0.40 halves every modeled signal, so detection occurs closer to the object, or perhaps not within the simulated range. Lowering the threshold makes the robot classify more situations as close, which may cause earlier detection but can also create false alarms. The simulation is not a hardware driver. A real robot would replace the calculated signal with a voltage or digital value read from an actual sensor.
