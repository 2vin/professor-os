Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 14, Build a Virtual Line Follower. RoboRover has learned to measure wheel motion with encoders. Encoders tell us how far a wheel has turned, but wheel motion alone does not tell RoboRover where a visible path is. Today, we give RoboRover three downward-facing reflectance sensors and ask it to follow a dark line on a light floor. We will build a virtual version first. That lets us change one variable at a time and inspect what happens. The class connects three ideas: calibration, thresholding, and steering. Calibration means obtaining representative readings from the light floor and the dark line. Thresholding means turning each measurement into a simple decision: line detected, or line not detected. Steering means converting those decisions into an actuator command. The repeated process is a simple closed-loop feedback controller: measure, classify, steer, move, and measure again. In this course, we call repeated measurement and correction sustained feedback regulation. The next class will examine error, stability, oscillation, and controller design in more detail. Imagine RoboRover moving over black tape. Under its front edge are three sensors: left, center, and right. Each sensor shines light toward the floor and measures reflected light. A light floor reflects more light. A dark line reflects less light. The physical light signal varies continuously, so it is analogue. The electronics then sample and quantize that signal with an analogue-to-digital converter, or ADC. The program works with a finite digital count, not with the original continuous signal. Suppose the ADC produces values from zero to 1023. In this hypothetical convention, zero is very dark and 1023 is very bright. A simple rule might be: line detected if the reading is less than 500. That comparison is a threshold. It turns a detailed, quantized measurement into a binary decision. If a reading is below 500, the program reports line detected. If it is 500 or higher, it reports floor. But do not assume that every real sensor behaves this way. Some sensors produce larger values for darker surfaces. The direction of the comparison must be checked for the actual hardware. Let’s work through one example. Suppose the three readings are 720, 310, and 690, for left, center, and right. With a threshold of 500, the left reading is not below the threshold, the center reading is below it, and the right reading is not below it. The binary state is zero, one, zero. The center sensor detects the line, so RoboRover drives straight. Now suppose the readings are 420, 760, and 780. The binary state is one, zero, zero. The left sensor sees the line, so RoboRover should make a small correction to the left. Here is a pause and predict question. If the readings are 700, 250, and 680, which sensor detects the line, assuming a threshold of 500? Brief beat. The center sensor detects the line, because 250 is less than 500. RoboRover should drive straight. To make steering unambiguous, we establish a coordinate convention. The robot’s centreline is x equals zero. Positive x points to the robot’s right, and negative x points to its left. If the robot centre is at sideways position x, and the sensor offset is S, then the left, center, and right sensors are at x minus S, x, and x plus S. A deterministic policy must define every three-sensor state. If only the left sensor detects the line, steer left. If only the right sensor detects it, steer right. If the center sensor detects it, drive straight. If both outer sensors detect it, also drive straight in this lesson. The same straight decision is used when the center and one outer sensor detect the line, or when all three detect it. Those cases can represent a wide line or an intersection. If no sensor detects the line, RoboRover continues in its previous search direction. That is a practical choice, not a guarantee that the line will be found again. At an actual intersection, the correct action depends on the route: the robot might need to go left, right, straight, or stop. Driving straight here is only a deterministic demonstration policy. In a real differential-drive robot, steering left could mean making the left wheel slower and the right wheel faster. Steering right reverses that relationship. The unequal wheel speeds change the robot’s heading and make its forward path curve. The virtual model deliberately simplifies this. It does not simulate heading, wheel rotation, turning radius, wheel dynamics, slip, or motor delay. Instead, it changes the robot’s sideways position directly by a small amount. That means the virtual robot can move sideways instantaneously. This is not a physically realizable vehicle path, but it keeps our attention on sensing, classification, and feedback. For calibration, suppose light-floor readings are 780 and dark-line readings are 220. A simple midpoint threshold is 500, because 780 plus 220, divided by two, equals 500. A later reading of 430 is classified as line, while a reading of 620 is classified as floor. The threshold is not magic. If the floor becomes darker, the line becomes dusty, or lighting changes, the same threshold may fail. A midpoint is a useful first estimate when the readings are well separated, but real calibration should use representative samples and consider noise and changing illumination. The Python lab models this process conceptually. The program calibrates a threshold from several floor and line samples, evaluates a curved virtual line, calculates the three sensor positions, classifies their readings, chooses one of three steering commands, and updates the virtual robot position. It repeats this for discrete forward samples. The supplied calibration samples produce a threshold of 500. The robot starts at sideways position 0.040 metres while the line initially is at zero. With a sensor offset of 0.045 metres, the left sensor is at negative 0.005 metres, the center sensor at 0.040, and the right sensor at 0.085. The left sensor detects the line first, so the first steering command is left, represented by minus one.
