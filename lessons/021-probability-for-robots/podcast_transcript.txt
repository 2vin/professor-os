Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 21: Probability for Robots. RoboRover already uses odometry to estimate movement. It counts wheel rotations and converts them into distance and turning. For a simple wheeled robot, distance is estimated as the number of wheel rotations multiplied by wheel circumference, and circumference is pi multiplied by wheel diameter. That calculation is useful, but it is not perfectly reliable. A wheel may slip on dust. The measured diameter may be slightly wrong. The floor may be uneven. Two journeys with the same motor command may end in slightly different places. So instead of saying, “RoboRover moved exactly 1.00 metres,” we can say, “RoboRover probably moved close to 1.00 metres, but several distances are possible.” That shift is the beginning of probabilistic robotics: representing uncertainty with numbers instead of pretending every measurement is exact. Let’s use a simple model. Imagine RoboRover is commanded to travel from a blue starting line to a red marker exactly 1.00 metre away. It might travel 0.80 metres if the wheels slip, 1.00 metre if the odometry is accurate, or 1.20 metres if calibration causes an overshoot. Suppose the probabilities are 0.20 for 0.80 metres, 0.50 for 1.00 metre, and 0.30 for 1.20 metres. These probabilities add to 1.00, as a complete distribution should. This is a discrete, or discretized, model. Real distance is continuous: the robot could stop at 0.93 metres, 1.07 metres, or many other values. We are grouping the possibilities into three representative outcomes so they are easy to calculate and visualize. An outcome is one possible result, such as 1.00 metre. An event is a collection of outcomes that matches a condition we care about. For example, the event “RoboRover travels at least 1.00 metre” includes both 1.00 and 1.20 metres. Its probability is 0.50 plus 0.30, which equals 0.80. A probability distribution assigns likelihoods to possible outcomes. It does not mean the robot travels all three distances at once. It describes our uncertainty before we observe the result. Pause and predict: in this example, which distance has the tallest probability bar: 0.80, 1.00, or 1.20 metres? Brief beat. The answer is 1.00 metre, because its probability is 0.50, the largest of the three. But most likely does not mean certain. A probability of 0.50 means that outcome is expected about half the time across repeated comparable trials, according to this model. Now let’s calculate the expected value. The expected value is a probability-weighted average. In plain language, multiply each possible distance by its probability, then add the results. For our model, that is 0.20 times 0.80 metres, plus 0.50 times 1.00 metre, plus 0.30 times 1.20 metres. Those contributions are 0.16 metres, 0.50 metres, and 0.36 metres. Add them, and the expected distance is 1.02 metres. Notice something important: 1.02 metres is not one of the listed outcomes. The expected value is the center of the distribution, not a guaranteed stopping point and not necessarily a result the robot will actually produce. We can also describe spread. Standard deviation is a model-dependent measure of how widely outcomes are scattered around the expected value. For this distribution, the standard deviation is 0.14 metres, or 14 centimetres. So the model tells us three useful things. The most likely single result is 1.00 metre. The expected distance is 1.02 metres. And the modeled spread is 0.14 metres. That spread is not a guarantee that most individual results will fall within exactly 14 centimetres of the expected value. It is a summary of this particular model. This gives us a memorable engineering example. If RoboRover is trying to stop near a charging pad only a few centimetres wide, odometry alone may not be reliable enough. The uncertainty suggests that the robot may need another measurement or a correction step near the pad. The most important engineering caveat is that a probability model is useful, but it is not automatically true. The model must match the operating conditions. A distribution measured on a smooth laboratory floor may be a poor model on carpet, a ramp, or dusty ground. There may also be bias. If the wheels are slightly larger than the value used by the odometry calculation, RoboRover may consistently travel farther than expected. That is systematic error. Random probability alone cannot repair a badly calibrated robot. Also, repeated trials are not always perfectly independent. A warm motor, changing battery voltage, or a patch of dust can make consecutive results similar. In the Python lab, you will represent the three distances and their probabilities, then simulate many drives by drawing weighted random outcomes. The program calculates the theoretical expected value and standard deviation, counts how often each outcome appears, and compares the theoretical probabilities with simulated relative frequencies in a chart. The simulated average will probably be near 1.02 metres, but it is unlikely to be exactly 1.02 metres every time. With more trials, the simulated average and frequencies will generally become more representative of the theoretical model, although random variation may not improve smoothly with every increase. You can also try this without Python. Assign 20 random trials to three landing zones: 0.80, 1.00, and 1.20 metres. Use numbers from 0 to 19 for the short outcome, 20 to 69 for the middle outcome, and 70 to 99 for the long outcome. Record the results, calculate observed frequencies, and then repeat with 100 trials. Your observed frequencies will not match 0.20, 0.50, and 0.30 exactly. That is normal. A probability describes a long-run pattern, not a promise about the next individual trial. For a practical challenge, calculate the probability that RoboRover travels more than 1.00 metre. “More than” excludes the boundary value, so only 1.20 metres counts. The answer is 0.30. By contrast, “at least 1.00 metre” includes 1.00 and 1.20 metres, giving 0.80. A second sensor measurement near the charging pad could help estimate the robot’s actual position and correct an odometry error before stopping. Let’s recap. Odometry provides an estimate, not certainty.
