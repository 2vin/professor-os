Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 22, Sensor Noise. RoboRover has learned that robots do not receive perfect information. In the previous class, Probability for Robots, we described uncertainty using ideas such as average value, spread, and likelihood. Today, we apply those ideas to a practical robotics problem: RoboRover measures the same object several times, but the readings do not agree exactly. That disagreement is called sensor noise. A sensor does not hand the robot the truth. It provides a measurement related to the truth. A simple measurement model is: z equals x plus e. Here, z is the measured value, x is the actual physical value, and e is the measurement error. All three use the same units. For example, suppose a wall is really 100 centimeters away, but the sensor reports 103 centimeters. The error is the measurement minus the true value: 103 minus 100, or positive 3 centimeters. Measurement error can come from electronic interference, surface texture, changing light, vibration, rounding, or limitations in the sensing method. The robot can still operate successfully, as long as its software understands that measurements are uncertain. A useful first question is this: are the errors scattered around the truth, or are they consistently pulled in one direction? Scattered errors suggest random error. A consistent shift suggests bias, or calibration error. Imagine RoboRover is stationary 100 centimeters from a wall. It records five readings: 98, 103, 101, 99, and 100 centimeters. The corresponding errors are minus 2, plus 3, plus 1, minus 1, and zero centimeters. The errors have different signs and sizes, so some of them may cancel when we average the readings. The arithmetic mean is the sum of the readings divided by five. The sum is 501 centimeters, so the average is 100.2 centimeters. That is close to the assumed true distance, even though no individual reading is perfect. This is the central idea behind filtering. Filtering transforms sensor data into a more useful signal. It can reduce rapid fluctuations, make an estimate more stable, and prevent motors from reacting to every tiny change. Consider RoboRover trying to stop 50 centimeters from a blue storage box. Its readings jump between 49, 52, 48, 51, and 50 centimeters. The box did not rapidly move back and forth. The sensor is imperfect. If the robot changed its motor command dramatically after every reading, it might move forward, backward, forward, and backward even though the box had not moved. A filter combines or transforms measurements so that random variation has less immediate influence on the robot’s working estimate. One simple weighted filter is called first-order exponential smoothing, or a one-pole low-pass filter. In this class, we can simply call it a weighted filter. It uses the newest measurement and the previous filtered estimate: The new filtered value equals alpha times the newest raw measurement, plus one minus alpha times the previous filtered value. Alpha is a unitless weight between zero and one. A larger alpha trusts the newest reading more. A smaller alpha creates stronger smoothing, but usually more delay. Let’s return to the five readings around 100 centimeters. Use alpha equal to 0.4, and begin the filtered estimate at the first reading, 98 centimeters. The second filtered value is 0.4 times 103, plus 0.6 times 98. That equals 100 centimeters. The third value is 0.4 times 101, plus 0.6 times 100, giving 100.4 centimeters. The fourth is 0.4 times 99, plus 0.6 times 100.4, giving 99.84 centimeters. The fifth is 0.4 times 100, plus 0.6 times 99.84, giving 99.904 centimeters. Notice what happened. The raw readings moved sharply from 98 to 103, while the filtered estimate changed more gradually. Pause and predict: if alpha is 0.25, the newest reading is 101 centimeters, and the previous filtered estimate is 100 centimeters, what is the new filtered value? Brief pause. The answer is 0.25 times 101, plus 0.75 times 100. That equals 100.25 centimeters. The newest reading contributes one quarter of the result, while the previous estimate contributes three quarters. Now for the most important engineering caveat: a smooth measurement can still be inaccurate. Suppose the true distance is 100 centimeters, but a poorly calibrated sensor repeatedly reports values near 106 centimeters. The readings might be 105, 106, 106, 107, and 106. A filter can make those values even smoother, but it will not automatically remove the approximately 6-centimeter positive bias. Calibration and filtering solve different problems. Calibration attempts to correct systematic error. Filtering attempts to reduce variation, especially random variation. Averaging and smoothing also work best only under suitable conditions: the errors should be reasonably random, not strongly correlated, and the true value should remain nearly constant. Filtering can fail or become misleading when errors follow the robot’s motion, come from repeating vibration or reflections, contain large outliers, or occur while the target is changing. This creates a basic trade-off. A low alpha gives more smoothing and usually more lag. A high alpha gives faster response but allows more noise through. If a box suddenly moves from 100 centimeters to 70 centimeters, a heavily smoothed estimate may take time to catch up. That delay could matter for obstacle detection or other safety-related decisions. The Python lab makes this behavior visible. Conceptually, the program first verifies the five-reading example, then generates simulated noisy measurements around a fixed 100-centimeter distance, applies the weighted filter, and plots the raw readings beside the filtered estimate. A fixed random seed makes the simulation repeatable. You can change alpha from 0.25 to 0.75 or 0.05 and compare responsiveness with smoothness. The simulation uses artificial noise, not measurements from real hardware. On a real RoboRover, engineers would measure a stationary target repeatedly, examine the average and spread, check for consistent bias, and test whether errors change with motor speed, lighting, target surface, or robot motion. They would test both steady conditions and sudden changes. A slow inspection rover might prefer a stable estimate. A fast obstacle-avoidance system might need a quicker response and tolerate more noise.
