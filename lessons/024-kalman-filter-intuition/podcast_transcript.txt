Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 24, Kalman Filter Intuition. In the previous class, we used moving average filters to reduce random sensor noise by combining recent measurements. That works well when the important value changes slowly, but RoboRover now needs something more flexible. Imagine the rover driving down a straight corridor. Its wheel encoders respond quickly and help predict how far it has moved, but the prediction can drift because of wheel slip or an imperfect speed estimate. Occasionally, a distance sensor measures the rover’s position relative to a landmark. That sensor does not drift in the same way, but its readings are noisy and may arrive less often. A Kalman filter combines these two sources of information by repeating two actions: prediction and correction. First, prediction uses a motion model to estimate what should happen next. Then, correction compares that prediction with a new measurement and moves the estimate toward the measurement according to how uncertain each source is. That is the central idea: a Kalman filter combines a model-based prediction, a sensor measurement, and uncertainty estimates for both. We will use a scalar Kalman filter. Scalar simply means that we estimate one quantity at a time. Here, that quantity is RoboRover’s position along a straight corridor. Real robotic filters often estimate several quantities together, such as position and velocity, but the same reasoning is visible in this smaller example. Here is a physical analogy. Suppose two people are estimating the location of a hidden treasure. One person says, “I walked ten meters, then another two point four meters, and I am fairly confident.” The other uses a noisy tape measure and says, “The treasure is at eleven meters, but my measurement is somewhat uncertain.” You would not automatically choose one answer. You would trust the more reliable source more strongly. A Kalman filter performs that compromise mathematically. The filter stores an estimated position, called x, and an uncertainty represented by variance, called P. During prediction, the new estimated position equals the old position plus estimated velocity multiplied by elapsed time. In plain language, position advances according to the motion model. The uncertainty also changes. Predicted variance equals the old variance plus process variance, called Q. Process variance represents uncertainty added by imperfect motion: wheel slip, uneven flooring, an inaccurate speed estimate, or similar effects. So prediction usually makes the uncertainty cloud wider. When a measurement arrives, the filter begins correction. It first calculates the Kalman gain. The gain is predicted variance divided by predicted variance plus measurement variance, called R. The gain is between zero and one in this simple case. A gain near one means trust the measurement more. A gain near zero means trust the prediction more. Importantly, the gain is not a permanent setting. It changes as the uncertainties change. Next, the filter calculates the innovation. That is the measurement minus the predicted position. The innovation tells us how much the measurement disagrees with the prediction. Finally, the new position equals the predicted position plus the gain multiplied by the innovation. The updated variance is one minus the gain, multiplied by the predicted variance. In general, correction narrows the uncertainty cloud. Let’s work through one complete example. RoboRover’s previous estimated position is ten meters, with a previous variance of four square meters. It estimates a velocity of one point two meters per second and moves for two seconds. The process variance is one square meter. Then the distance sensor reports eleven meters, with a measurement variance of four square meters. First, predict the position. Ten meters plus one point two meters per second times two seconds gives twelve point four meters. Next, predict the uncertainty. Four square meters plus one square meter gives five square meters. The uncertainty increased because motion is imperfect. Now calculate the gain. It is five divided by five plus four, or five-ninths, approximately zero point five five five six. The innovation is the measurement, eleven meters, minus the prediction, twelve point four meters. That gives negative one point four meters. The sensor is one point four meters behind the prediction. The corrected position is twelve point four plus zero point five five five six times negative one point four. The result is approximately eleven point six two meters. The updated variance is approximately two point two two square meters. So the estimate does not jump all the way to eleven meters. The motion prediction still contains useful information, so the filter moves partway toward the sensor reading. At the same time, uncertainty decreases from five square meters to about two point two two square meters. Pause and predict: if the predicted uncertainty is large while the measurement uncertainty is small, should the Kalman gain be closer to zero or closer to one? Let’s give that a brief beat. The answer is closer to one. A large prediction uncertainty and a small measurement uncertainty mean the filter should trust the measurement more strongly. In the Python lab, you will simulate a one-dimensional robot moving repeatedly. The simulation creates a hidden true position, adds noise to produce synthetic sensor measurements, and then runs prediction and correction cycles. A plot shows the hidden true position, the scattered noisy measurements, and the filtered estimate. You can experiment by changing the measurement variance. With a small measurement variance, the estimate should follow sensor points more closely, though it may look noisier. With a large measurement variance, the filter should rely more on the motion model, producing a smoother estimate that may drift if the model is wrong. The most important engineering caveat is that uncertainty is a model, not a guarantee. A smaller variance does not prove that a sensor is accurate. A sensor can be confidently wrong because of calibration failure, an unexpected obstacle, or an incorrect assumption. For example, suppose the range sensor is consistently two meters too high because of a calibration error. The Kalman filter may repeatedly pull RoboRover toward the wrong position.
