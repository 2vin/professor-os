Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 26, Thresholding and Segmentation. In the previous class, RoboRover learned that an image is a grid of numbers. In grayscale, each pixel has one brightness value. Today, we begin interpreting those measurements by asking: which pixels probably belong to the thing RoboRover cares about? Our main tool is thresholding. Thresholding turns a grayscale image into a binary mask, separating pixels into two groups. The foreground is the selected group, perhaps a bright strip of tape. The background is everything not selected. For an 8-bit grayscale image, intensity values commonly range from zero to 255. Zero is at the dark end of the chosen scale, and 255 is very bright. These are camera measurements, not direct statements about whether an object exists. Lighting, exposure, reflections, lens behavior, and electronic noise can all affect them. Suppose RoboRover is looking for bright tape on a dark floor. Instead of treating every brightness value as equally important, it chooses a cutoff called the threshold, written as T. For each pixel, it asks: is this intensity greater than or equal to T? If the answer is yes, the mask stores a one, meaning foreground. If the answer is no, it stores a zero, meaning background. With a threshold of 150, an intensity of 149 becomes zero, while 150 and 220 become one. That equality rule matters. This simple process is called thresholding. Segmentation is the broader task of dividing an image into meaningful regions, and thresholding is one possible segmentation method. Think of the mask as a black-and-white map. Black represents zero, and white represents one. A connected white patch might be the tape RoboRover wants. Small isolated white dots might instead be noise or reflections. The mask deliberately discards information. It no longer remembers whether a selected pixel had intensity 151 or 240. Both become one. That loss can help later algorithms work with a clear region, but it can also be dangerous. A poor threshold may miss part of the target, include unrelated bright objects, split one object into pieces, or join separate objects together. Here is a worked example. Imagine this four-row by five-column grayscale image. The rows contain these values: first, 20, 35, 40, 180, and 210. Second, 25, 45, 155, 190, and 205. Third, 30, 50, 60, 170, and 220. Fourth, 15, 40, 130, 160, and 200. Use a threshold of 150. Every value at least 150 becomes one, and every lower value becomes zero. The resulting mask has rows: zero, zero, zero, one, one; then zero, zero, one, one, one; then zero, zero, zero, one, one; and finally zero, zero, zero, one, one. Count the ones. There are nine foreground pixels. Now suppose each pixel represents a two-millimetre by two-millimetre square on an approximately flat surface. Each pixel therefore represents four square millimetres. Nine foreground pixels give an estimated area of 36 square millimetres. This is only an approximation. A pixel is an image sample, not a physical tile permanently attached to the world. The pixel-to-millimetre scale depends on camera geometry. For a downward-facing camera over a flat surface, a calibrated central region might use a scale of two millimetres per pixel. With perspective, pixels in different parts of the image may represent different physical areas. The area formula is most reasonable when the surface is roughly flat and the camera geometry is known. Pause and predict: if RoboRover raises the threshold, will more pixels or fewer pixels become foreground? Brief beat. Fewer pixels. A higher threshold makes the selection rule stricter. Under this simple rule, increasing the threshold cannot turn a rejected pixel into a selected one. That monotonic behavior is useful, but it does not make threshold choice automatic. A threshold is a hypothesis: brightness is being used as evidence that a pixel belongs to the target. That assumption works when target and background brightness remain reliably different. It becomes weak when the target is shadowed, the floor has bright patches, or illumination changes across the image. The Python lab turns this idea into an experiment. It creates a simulated grayscale camera image with a darker floor, a bright circular region, a dimmer circular region, and a separate bright stripe. You can adjust a slider and watch the binary mask update. The program also verifies the nine-pixel example and calculates the percentage of foreground pixels. At a threshold near 150, the bright region with intensity 205 is selected, while the dimmer region at 135 and the stripe, whose values are approximately 70 to 94, are rejected. At a threshold of 210, even the bright region disappears, because 205 does not meet the cutoff. This gives us two important error types. A false positive is a selected pixel that does not belong to the intended target, such as a bright floor reflection. A false negative is a target pixel that was rejected. At threshold 210, pixels belonging to the bright region become false negatives. The most important engineering caveat is lighting and camera exposure. Automatic exposure can change the numerical intensity of the same physical marker from one frame to the next. A threshold that works in a bright laboratory may fail in a shadow. Practical systems may use controlled lighting, camera calibration, thresholds tested across expected conditions, thresholds that adapt to local brightness, or evidence beyond brightness. None of these is a guarantee. Also remember that grayscale can discard useful color information. A red object and a green object may have similar grayscale brightness. If color distinguishes the target, a color-based threshold may be more appropriate. Thresholding an RGB channel is not the same as thresholding hue, saturation, or value in HSV. Each representation measures something different and produces a different selection rule. In the real robot connection, a mask might help RoboRover find a bright landing marker, a colored inspection label, or a high-contrast strip. But the mask tells us only which pixels passed the rule. It does not yet provide the exact boundary, center, orientation, or distance.
