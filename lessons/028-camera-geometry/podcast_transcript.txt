Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 28, Camera Geometry. RoboRover already knows how to turn camera images into useful shapes. In the previous class, Contours and Shape Detection, it could trace the boundary of a bright orange marker. But a camera image is not a flat map of the world. A large object nearby can look the same size as a small object far away. A hallway appears to narrow toward the horizon. Objects near the edge of an image may look stretched because of the lens. To reason about these effects, RoboRover needs camera geometry: the relationship between three-dimensional objects in the world and their two-dimensional positions and sizes in an image. Today we will focus on two connected ideas. First is projection: how a three-dimensional point becomes a two-dimensional image point. Second is field of view: how much of the world the camera can see. The central idea is simple. A camera maps three coordinates into two. A point in space has sideways position, vertical position, and forward distance. We can call those coordinates X, Y, and Z. The image records a horizontal pixel coordinate, u, and a vertical pixel coordinate, v. So the camera maps X, Y, Z into u and v. In the process, some depth information is lost. Different three-dimensional points can land on the same image location. That is why one ordinary image usually cannot tell you both an object’s true size and its distance without additional information. A useful mental model is the pinhole camera. Imagine a tiny opening, straight light rays, and a flat image surface. This is not a complete model of a real lens, but it is an excellent first approximation. In the usual computer-vision convention, Z points forward from the camera, X points to the camera’s right, and image rows commonly increase downward. The pixel projection equations are: u equals f sub x times X divided by Z, plus c sub x; and v equals f sub y times Y divided by Z, plus c sub y. Here, f sub x and f sub y are effective focal lengths measured in pixels. The terms c sub x and c sub y describe the optical center, also called the principal point. The division by Z is the important part. As distance increases, the projected position relative to the optical center and the apparent size become smaller. If a physical coordinate system treats vertical Y as positive upward instead of following the image-row convention, the vertical equation needs a minus sign. The equation is only meaningful when its coordinate convention is clearly declared. Let’s work through a numerical example. Suppose RoboRover’s horizontal focal length is 400 pixels, and the optical center is at horizontal pixel coordinate 320. An orange beacon is half a metre to the right of the camera’s center line and two metres in front of the camera. The horizontal pixel coordinate is 400 times 0.50 divided by 2.00, plus 320. The ratio is 0.25, so 400 times 0.25 is 100. Add 320, and the beacon appears at pixel coordinate 420. That means it appears 100 pixels to the right of the optical center. The optical center matters because pixel zero is the image edge, not the camera’s forward direction. This same geometry explains apparent size. If a marker is 0.30 metres tall, the vertical focal length is 400 pixels, and the marker is two metres away, its approximate image height is 400 times 0.30 divided by 2.00, which equals 60 pixels. Pause and predict: if that same marker moves from two metres away to four metres away, will its image height stay at 60 pixels, become 30 pixels, or become 120 pixels? Take a brief beat. The answer is 30 pixels. Doubling the distance halves the projected size, assuming the marker’s physical height and orientation stay the same. This relationship can estimate distance when the object’s true size is known. Rearranging the formula gives distance equal to focal length times physical height divided by image height. But this estimate is only approximate. The object should be nearly fronto-parallel, meaning its measured dimension faces the camera rather than being strongly tilted. Now consider field of view, or FOV. This is the angular width or height visible to the camera. Horizontal field of view equals two times the inverse tangent of sensor width divided by twice the focal length. For example, with a sensor width of 6.4 millimetres and a focal length of 4.0 millimetres, the horizontal field of view is approximately 77.3 degrees. A wider sensor produces a wider view. A longer focal length produces a narrower view. A wide-angle camera sees more area, but spreads that area across the same image width, so distant objects may occupy fewer pixels. A narrow-angle camera sees less area but can give more image detail to distant objects. This is a geometric tradeoff, not a complete statement about image quality. There is an important distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic parameters. Intrinsic parameters describe how the camera forms image coordinates: focal lengths, optical center, and related calibration values. Extrinsic parameters describe where the camera is and how it is rotated relative to the robot or world. The projection equations require coordinates in the camera frame. If a point is given in a world or robot frame, RoboRover must first apply the camera’s rotation and translation. Substituting world coordinates directly into the projection equation is a common and serious mistake. The Python lab models a camera viewing a vertical orange marker. It calculates projected image height, horizontal and vertical field of view, and draws a two-dimensional side view of the camera’s viewing wedge. It also uses assertions to verify the expected values. The lab invites you to change distance, marker height, and focal length, predict the result, and then compare your prediction with the calculation. It demonstrates that projected height increases with physical height and focal length, and decreases with distance.
