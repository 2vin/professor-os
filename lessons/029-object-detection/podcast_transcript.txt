Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 29: Object Detection. RoboRover has just learned camera geometry. Its camera produces an image made of pixels, and those pixels can be related to directions in the world. But geometry alone cannot tell RoboRover what it is looking at. A geometry system might say, “This bright shape is 120 pixels from the image center.” Object detection adds a more useful answer: “There is probably a blue supply crate in this rectangular image region, and the detector is fairly confident.” Today’s central idea is that an object-detection result combines three kinds of information: where an object appears, what kind of object it may be, and how strongly the detector supports that conclusion. The first part is the bounding box. A box is usually described by four pixel coordinates: the left edge, the top edge, the right edge, and the bottom edge. In image coordinates, x increases to the right, and y usually increases downward. The second part is the class. A class is a category from the detector’s known vocabulary, such as crate, cone, beacon, or person. A class does not tell us an object’s distance, orientation, or physical size. It only identifies the category the detector believes fits best. The third part is confidence. This is a numerical score indicating how strongly the detector supports a proposed class-and-box result. Scores are often written between zero and one. A score of zero point ninety-five represents stronger support than zero point fifty-five, while zero point ten represents weak support. But confidence is evidence, not certainty. A score of zero point eighty does not automatically mean there is an exactly eighty percent chance the result is correct. Its meaning depends on the detector, its training data, evaluation method, and operating conditions. Let’s work through one example. Suppose the camera image is 640 pixels wide and 480 pixels high. A detector reports a crate in a box whose left edge is at 180 pixels, whose top edge is at 120 pixels, whose right edge is at 340 pixels, and whose bottom edge is at 300 pixels. Its confidence is zero point eighty-six. The box width is 340 minus 180, or 160 pixels. Its height is 300 minus 120, or 180 pixels. To find the center, average the left and right edges. That gives 260 pixels horizontally. Average the top and bottom edges, and the vertical center is 210 pixels. So the crate’s image center is 260 pixels across and 210 pixels down. The image center is 320 pixels across and 240 pixels down. Therefore, the crate appears 60 pixels left of the image center and 30 pixels above it. That tells RoboRover where the crate appears in the image. It does not, by itself, tell RoboRover the crate’s distance in meters. A box width of 160 pixels is an image measurement, not a physical width. The box area is also easy to calculate: 160 times 180, or 28,800 square pixels. Again, square pixels describe the image region, not the physical area of the crate. Now suppose RoboRover uses a confidence threshold of zero point sixty. Because zero point eighty-six is greater than or equal to zero point sixty, this crate candidate passes the threshold. Imagine a second candidate, also labeled crate, with confidence zero point forty-two. RoboRover rejects it under this rule. That does not prove no crate exists. It only means the current detection system does not have enough support to accept that candidate under the chosen threshold. Pause and predict: if the detector also reports a cone with confidence zero point seventy-three and a beacon with confidence zero point forty-two, how many candidates will remain at a threshold of zero point sixty? Brief beat. The answer is two: the crate and the cone. The beacon is rejected because zero point forty-two is below zero point sixty. This threshold is a decision rule, not a truth detector. A high threshold can reduce weak false alarms, but it may also miss real objects. A low threshold can preserve more possible objects, but it may produce more false alarms. The appropriate threshold depends on the task. Missing a person near a moving robot is more serious than displaying an extra box in a harmless monitoring application. The Python lab demonstrates this idea with a small artificial scene. It stores each candidate’s class, box coordinates, and confidence, then applies a threshold. It calculates the accepted crate’s center and draws solid boxes for accepted candidates and faint, dashed boxes for rejected ones. This is not a trained vision model. It is a transparent simulation for practicing boxes, classes, confidence, and image-coordinate conventions. You can change the threshold and predict what happens. At zero point eighty, only the crate remains. At zero point fifty, the crate and cone remain. At zero point thirty, all three candidates remain. Changing the threshold changes which candidates the program uses; it does not change the boxes themselves. Several engineering caveats matter. First, coordinate formats must not be confused. Some systems use left, top, right, bottom. Others use center x, center y, width, and height. Mixing these formats can produce boxes in absurd locations or with negative dimensions. Second, remember the image convention. On ordinary mathematical graphs, y often increases upward. In most image arrays and displays, y increases downward. A vertical sign mistake can make a rover steer in the wrong direction. Third, a bounding box is only an approximation. It may include a little floor, shadow, or background, and partial visibility can make it smaller or poorly positioned. Finally, pixel measurements are not physical measurements. Apparent size depends on distance, perspective, and camera geometry. Lighting changes, glare, motion blur, vibration, unusual object appearance, and processing delay can all make detections less reliable. The practical takeaway is to treat detection as structured evidence. For every result, ask: where is the box, what class was assigned, and how strong is the confidence?
