Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 30, Tracking Objects Over Time. In the previous class, RoboRover learned object detection. Its camera could examine one image and report, for example, that there is a red toolbox, that its center is at a particular image position, and that its bounding box has a certain size. That is useful, but one image cannot tell RoboRover whether the toolbox is moving, where it was one second ago, or whether the toolbox in the next image is the same toolbox. Today, we add memory. Tracking means comparing detections from one moment with detections from the next. It has two central ideas: association and motion. Association decides which new detection belongs to which existing tracked object. Motion uses recent movement to predict where that object should appear next. A detector answers, “What appears to be present now?” A tracker asks, “Which current observation continues which earlier object, and where will it probably be next?” A track is a temporary record. It may contain an identifier, the latest measured or predicted position, an estimated velocity, the number of frames since a successful detection, and a history of earlier positions. That identifier is only a working label. Track 3 is not a permanent name engraved on the object. Let’s use a one-dimensional example, such as distance along a calibrated rail. Suppose RoboRover previously measured a toolbox at 2.4 metres. Its estimated velocity is 0.8 metres per second, and half a second has passed. The predicted position is the old position plus velocity times elapsed time: 2.4 metres plus 0.8 metres per second times 0.5 seconds. That gives 2.8 metres. Now the detector reports two possible positions: Detection A at 2.9 metres and Detection B at 4.1 metres. The signed prediction residual is the measurement minus the prediction. For A, that is 2.9 minus 2.8, or positive 0.1 metres. For B, it is 4.1 minus 2.8, or positive 1.3 metres. Suppose the matching threshold is 0.6 metres. Detection A is close enough to be accepted for this track. Detection B is rejected. After accepting A, a simple new velocity estimate is the change in measured position divided by elapsed time: 2.9 minus 2.4 metres, divided by 0.5 seconds. That is approximately 1.0 metre per second. Pause and predict: if the toolbox disappears from one camera frame, should RoboRover immediately delete its track? Briefly think about it. The answer is no, not necessarily. RoboRover can predict where the toolbox should be, keep the track for a short time, and wait for another detection. Its confidence is lower, but the object has not automatically ceased to exist. This is the basic tracking cycle: predict, receive detections, associate detections with predictions, update positions and motion, and repeat. Association is easy when objects are far apart. It becomes difficult when two objects come close, cross paths, move unpredictably, or look alike. A simple method is greedy nearest-neighbor matching: consider the closest available prediction and detection, accept that pair if it is within the threshold, and continue. This is useful for teaching, but it is order-dependent and can produce a worse overall assignment than a global matching method. For example, with predictions at 0 and 4 metres, and detections at 3 and 5 metres, a greedy method might let the 4-metre track claim the 3-metre detection first. The 0-metre track is then left unmatched, even though the better overall assignment is 0 to 3 and 4 to 5. Track management is also important. An unmatched detection can start a new track. A track with no matching detection can be kept briefly as a missed track and advanced through prediction. After repeated misses, it can be deleted. Deleting immediately may cause identity flicker. Keeping a stale track forever fills the system with objects that are no longer supported by evidence. The most important engineering caveat is that a wrong association can be worse than no association. A missed detection creates visible uncertainty: RoboRover knows it does not currently see the object. A wrong association silently attaches measurements to the wrong object. That false history can produce a bad velocity estimate and poor future predictions. Real systems may use confidence scores, appearance information, uncertainty estimates, and global assignment methods, but tracking always involves imperfect evidence. There is another essential coordinate warning. Image positions are often measured in pixels, such as an x-coordinate of 240 pixels. The examples today use metres along a rail or another calibrated one-dimensional coordinate. Pixels and world distances are not interchangeable. Converting an image position to metres requires calibration and, in general, depth or other geometric information. A pixel displacement cannot automatically be interpreted as a real-world displacement. In the Python lab, you will conceptually follow the complete tracking loop with two objects moving along a line. The program predicts each track, computes distances to detections, accepts close one-to-one matches, updates position and velocity, propagates unmatched tracks, deletes tracks after a configured number of missed frames, and creates tracks for unmatched detections. It also plots the trails. The lab demonstrates an important implementation detail. The current predicted position advances during an occlusion or missed frame, while the last measured position is preserved. When the object reappears, the velocity estimate accounts for the time since the last successful measurement rather than pretending that only one frame passed. With the baseline data, Track 1 finishes at 5.0 metres, Track 2 finishes at 4.0 metres, and the program reports eight successful associations. If one object is missing from the second frame, that track remains active, its predicted position advances, and its history records a gap. The program then reports seven successful associations. If the object is missed for two consecutive frames with the default setting, the track is deleted, and a later detection starts a new track. In a real robot, tracking also helps separate moving objects from fixed environmental features. A wall feature may move across the camera image as RoboRover drives, even though it is stationary in the world.
