Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 31: Mapping. In the previous class, RoboRover learned to track objects over time. Tracking asks a local question: where is a moving object now, and where might it be next? Mapping asks a broader question: what does the surrounding world look like, and where are important things located? A tracked object is usually one changing estimate. A map is a longer-lasting world model: a structured record of walls, open areas, obstacles, landmarks, and possibly moving objects. It helps RoboRover remember what it has seen, even after turning away. A sensor snapshot describes what the robot observes right now. A map describes what the robot currently believes is located in an area, based on observations collected over time. For example, a laser sensor might see a wall two point four metres ahead. That is one observation. RoboRover can place the wall into a map, combine it with earlier observations, and remember it later. A world model is not the physical world itself. It is an internal representation. It may be incomplete, noisy, outdated, or based on assumptions. For mapping, occupancy means whether a region of space is believed to contain something solid. A simple occupancy map uses three states. Occupied means the evidence suggests there is an obstacle or object. Free means the evidence suggests the region is open. Unknown means RoboRover does not yet have enough evidence. Unknown is especially important. If the robot has not looked behind a cabinet, it should not treat that space as safely empty. Unknown does not mean free. A map also needs a reference frame. In today’s two-dimensional map, the horizontal direction is called x, measured in metres. The vertical direction is y, also measured in metres. The origin is a chosen reference point, and the map has defined positive directions. RoboRover’s pose includes its x position, its y position, and its heading, written in ordinary speech as x, y, and theta. A sensor usually measures something relative to the robot. The map stores that observation in a common, fixed world frame. Here is the central idea: mapping combines robot-relative observations with the robot’s estimated pose to create a persistent world model. Let’s work through one example. Suppose RoboRover is at map position two metres along x and one metre along y. It detects a crate one point five metres directly in front of it. The robot is heading thirty degrees. To place the crate in the map, split that forward distance into horizontal and vertical parts. The horizontal part uses the cosine of thirty degrees, approximately zero point eight six six. The vertical part uses the sine of thirty degrees, which is zero point five. So the crate’s x position is two plus one point five times zero point eight six six, approximately three point three metres. Its y position is one plus one point five times zero point five, which is one point seven five metres. RoboRover therefore places the crate near map position three point three metres along x and one point seven five metres along y. Pause and predict. Is that location an exact fact, or an estimate? Brief beat. It is an estimate. The distance measurement may be off by several centimetres, and the robot’s heading may also be imperfect. A map is geometry plus evidence quality. Now consider a workshop exploration. At first, RoboRover sees the first two metres in front of it as clear. At two metres, an obstacle begins. The sensor cannot see beyond that obstacle. The map should record the nearby region as free, the obstacle boundary as occupied, and the region behind the obstacle as unknown. It should not automatically mark the hidden region as free. Later, RoboRover moves one metre sideways and looks again. From this new viewpoint, it can see around the obstacle. Some space behind it now appears clear, so that newly visible region can be updated to free. The map became more informative because the robot changed its viewpoint. Notice what the map still may not know. It may not know whether the obstacle is a crate or a wall. It may not know whether the object will move, whether a narrow gap is wide enough for the robot, or whether a shiny surface caused a false sensor reading. Maps can include stable structures, such as walls, floor boundaries, and support pillars. They can also include changing features, such as people, carts, doors, and boxes. A stale map can be dangerous if it remembers a cart that has moved or fails to record a newly placed box. The Python lab turns these ideas into a small top-down occupancy sketch. The program creates a rectangular map in which every cell begins unknown. It applies two batches of observations, marking cells as free or occupied, then counts the three states and displays them visually. The important programming idea is that a map cell is updated in place. If an observation repeats an already occupied cell, it does not create a second obstacle. The cell remains occupied. The map is stored as rows and columns, so the program uses the row index for y and the column index for x. After the two observation batches, the map contains four free cells, two occupied cells, and fifty-seven unknown cells. The repeated occupied observation does not increase the occupied count. This simple lab also highlights a limitation. A basic update may replace a previous value directly. Real mapping systems often combine evidence with confidence instead of allowing one questionable reading to erase a long history of reliable observations. The most important engineering caveat is that coordinate accuracy depends on more than arithmetic. Sensors are noisy. Robot movement estimates can be wrong. Timing delays and calibration errors matter. A small heading error can shift every detected obstacle to the wrong place, especially at longer distances. Sensors can also miss thin cables, transparent objects, dark or shiny surfaces, dust, or narrow obstacles, depending on the technology.
