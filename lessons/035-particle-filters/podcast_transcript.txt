Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 35, Particle Filters. In the previous class, RoboRover used Bayes Rule to update its belief about location. It began with a prior, received a sensor measurement, and calculated a new probability distribution. That works neatly when we can list every possible state. But a real robot may need to estimate its x and y position, its orientation, and perhaps other variables, across a large map. Motion and sensors are noisy, and several locations may look equally plausible. A particle filter handles this by representing belief with many random samples called particles. Each particle is a hypothesis: “Perhaps RoboRover is here.” In practice, particles are samples from an approximate belief distribution produced by motion and sensor models. They are not usually direct samples from the exact posterior distribution. The particle-filter cycle has three steps: predict, update, and resample. First, predict. Every particle moves according to the robot’s command and a noisy motion model. If RoboRover commands a two-metre move, one particle might move one point eight metres, another two point one, and another two point three. Second, update. The sensor measurement gives each particle a weight. A particle that predicts the observed measurement well receives a high weight. One that disagrees receives a low weight. Third, resample. The filter draws a new set of particles, selecting high-weight particles more often and low-weight particles less often. Resampling does not create new information. It reallocates computational attention toward hypotheses supported by the evidence. Imagine five hundred imaginary RoboRovers spread across a map. Initially, they may be scattered widely because the robot is uncertain. After motion and sensing, the cloud becomes concentrated where the evidence is strongest. If two different locations both explain the measurement, the cloud may form two groups. That is useful: the filter does not have to choose one answer too early. Let’s work through a simple example. RoboRover travels in a twelve-metre corridor. Blue markers are at five metres and eleven metres. Its colour sensor reports blue. The sensor reports blue with probability zero point eight at a blue marker, and with probability zero point one elsewhere. Before sensing, four candidate positions—two, five, eight, and eleven metres—are equally likely, each with probability zero point two five. The unnormalized weight is the prior multiplied by the likelihood. At five metres, that is zero point two five times zero point eight, or zero point two. At two metres, it is zero point two five times zero point one, or zero point zero two five. The same values apply at eleven and eight metres respectively. The total weight is zero point four five. So the updated probability at five metres is zero point two divided by zero point four five, approximately zero point four four four. Eleven metres has the same probability. The two other positions each have approximately zero point zero five six. The important conclusion is not that RoboRover is definitely at five metres. It is probably near one of the two blue markers, but the measurement does not distinguish between them. A particle filter can preserve both possibilities. Pause and predict: if we resample twelve particles, will every particle go to five metres, or will both blue-marker locations remain possible? Brief beat. Both remain possible. Each blue-marker location has probability four ninths, so the expected number near five metres is twelve times four ninths, about five point three. That is an expected count, not a guarantee. Random sampling might produce four, five, six, or another nearby number. Now consider the engineering caveat that matters most: particle impoverishment. Repeated resampling can make many particles identical. The filter then loses diversity and may struggle if the robot later moves into a different plausible region. Too few particles can cause a similar problem. If a plausible region receives no particles, ordinary resampling cannot recover it, because resampling selects only from particles that still exist. A misleading sensor model can also concentrate particles around the wrong location. Engineers address these risks with suitable motion noise, enough particles, improved resampling methods, recovery strategies, or a different estimator. None is an automatic guarantee. The lesson’s Python lab simulates a one-dimensional corridor with four hundred particles. Each particle is an integer position. The robot moves two cells per step with motion noise of minus one, zero, or plus one. A four-colour sensor reports the correct colour with likelihood zero point eight five and each alternative with likelihood zero point zero five. The program predicts particle motion, assigns sensor weights, resamples with weighted random choice, and plots the final particle distribution. The histogram shows where belief is concentrated. The red line marks the simulated true position, but the filter does not receive that position directly. The particle mean can be misleading when two clusters remain; it may fall between them, where few or no particles exist. The mode identifies the most frequently sampled position, but random sampling can change it. The program also calculates effective sample size, a rough diagnostic of how concentrated the weighted support was before resampling. The corridor code clamps particles at the boundaries. That is a simplifying simulation choice, not a universal motion model. In a real robot, walls, collisions, slipping, and invalid motions should be represented more realistically. A higher-dimensional state, including x, y, and orientation, also requires more particles for comparable accuracy. Remember the distinction between estimation, control, and planning. Estimation asks, “Where might I be?” Control asks, “What motor command should I send?” Planning asks, “Which route or action should I choose?” A particle filter estimates state; it does not directly control the motors. Let’s recap. A particle is one possible robot-state hypothesis. Predict moves particles using a noisy motion model. Update weights them according to sensor evidence. Resample copies likely hypotheses more often. Multiple clusters can represent genuine ambiguity, but too few particles, poor models, and repeated resampling can make hypotheses disappear or particles collapse together.
