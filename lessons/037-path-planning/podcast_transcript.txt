Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 37: Path Planning. In the previous class, RoboRover learned how to build a map while moving using SLAM, or simultaneous localization and mapping. SLAM estimates where the rover is, what parts of the environment may contain walls or objects, and how uncertain those estimates are. But a map alone does not tell RoboRover where to drive. Today, we use that map to plan a route from a start position to a goal position while avoiding obstacles. It helps to separate four related jobs. Mapping asks, “What is around me?” Localization asks, “Where am I on the map?” Planning asks, “Which route should I take?” And control asks, “How should I command the motors to follow that route?” These jobs interact, but they are not the same. A path-planning problem has three central ingredients: a start, a goal, and one or more obstacles. The start is RoboRover’s current or planned initial position. The goal is its destination, which might be one exact grid cell or a region near something such as a charging station. An obstacle is an area the rover should not enter or cross. It could be a wall, a crate, a person, a closed doorway, or an unsafe region. In a grid map, we can describe positions using coordinates. For example, the start might be at one comma one, written as “one, one,” and the goal might be at seven comma five. A path is an ordered sequence of positions beginning at the start and ending at the goal. For a four-direction grid, every consecutive pair of positions must be neighboring cells. Each move changes exactly one coordinate by one. In plain language, the rover may move one cell left, right, up, or down. It may not jump across several cells or move diagonally under this simplified model. A valid path therefore has three basic properties. It begins at the start and ends at the goal. Every consecutive pair is one valid grid move apart. And none of the listed path positions is an obstacle cell. Here is a short pause and predict question. Suppose a proposed path avoids every obstacle cell that it lists, but suddenly jumps from one side of the map to the other. Is it valid? Brief pause. No. It fails the movement check. A path must not only avoid obstacle cells; it must also connect neighboring positions through allowed movements. Otherwise, it could appear to bypass an obstacle simply by skipping over it. Now let’s work through one example. RoboRover is navigating a warehouse represented by grid cells that are each zero point five meters apart. The start is at one comma one, and the goal is at seven comma five. A rectangular obstacle occupies the cells with horizontal coordinates from three through five and vertical coordinates from two through four. A safe route begins at one comma one, moves right to two comma one, then moves upward through two comma two, two comma three, two comma four, and two comma five. From there, it moves right through three comma five, four comma five, five comma five, six comma five, and finally seven comma five. Let’s count the movements rather than the listed positions. There is one move right, four moves up, and five moves right. That gives ten total moves. Since each move represents zero point five meters, the planned center-to-center distance is ten times zero point five, or five point zero meters. This route stays to the left of the obstacle, rises above it, and then travels across to the goal. Notice that the route contains eleven listed positions but only ten movements between them. Distance is based on the connecting movements, not simply the number of positions. The Python lab models this process conceptually. The program stores the obstacle cells and the hand-designed path, checks that the path starts and ends in the correct places, checks every path cell for collisions, and verifies that every transition is a valid one-cell movement. It then counts the movements, multiplies by the cell size, and plots the route. The important point is that the program does not discover the route. A human supplies the path. The program validates and measures it. In the next class, graph search will begin replacing hand-designed routes with a systematic method for finding them. Now for the most important engineering caveat: a path that looks short and safe on a map may not be safe for a real robot. The grid example treats RoboRover as a point located at the center of one cell. A real rover has width and length. Even if its center passes very close to a wall without entering an obstacle cell, the body may still collide. Engineers handle this by reserving extra clearance around obstacles. This is called adding a safety margin, or obstacle inflation. The map itself is also uncertain. SLAM estimates walls and objects; it does not produce a perfectly accurate blueprint. A box may have moved, a wall may be slightly farther left or right than estimated, or a person may step into the planned route. For that reason, a practical rover repeatedly compares sensor measurements with its plan and may stop, update its map, or create a new route. There is another distinction worth remembering. The plotted route is a geometric path. It describes where the rover should go, but not exactly how fast the motors should turn or when the rover should accelerate and stop. Wheel slip, motor delays, turning arcs, and imperfect control can all make the real traveled distance longer than the planned distance. A lower-level motion controller must work to follow the path. A useful path may also consider more than distance. A planner might account for the number of turns, clearance from obstacles, battery use, time, and uncertainty in the map. For this introductory lesson, the basic requirements are simpler: connect start to goal, use valid movements, and avoid obstacles.
