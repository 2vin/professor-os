Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 38: Graph Search. RoboRover already knows that path planning means choosing a route through an environment. Previously, we described routes geometrically: move through free space while avoiding blocked regions. Today, we change the representation. Instead of treating the entire floor as one continuous picture, we describe important places as nodes and possible movements between them as edges. Each edge can have a cost, such as distance, travel time, battery energy, or risk. Together, these nodes and edges form a graph: a compact map that a computer can search. In the next class, we will study Dijkstra’s Algorithm, which finds a lowest-cost route when edge costs are nonnegative. Today, we are building the concepts that algorithm will use. A node, also called a vertex, represents a useful place, state, or situation. In a warehouse, a node might be the charging dock, an aisle intersection, a doorway, or the entrance to a loading bay. It might even represent a robot configuration, such as “the gripper is holding a box.” A node is not necessarily one exact physical point. It is a discrete description chosen because it helps solve the planning problem. An edge connects two nodes when the robot can move or transition between them. An edge might represent driving along a corridor, turning through an intersection, moving an arm between safe configurations, or taking an elevator. If RoboRover can travel both ways between two locations, the connection may be modeled as an undirected edge. If a narrow aisle allows travel in only one direction, it should be modeled as a directed edge. The graph has to reflect reality. Drawing an edge does not make the movement physically possible. The cost of an edge is the quantity we use to compare movements. It could be distance in meters, time in seconds, energy, money, risk, or a weighted combination. “Cost” does not automatically mean money. The cost of a complete route is usually the sum of the costs of its edges. If a route contains edges costing two meters, three meters, and four meters, the total is nine meters. The unit matters. If those numbers represented seconds instead, the total would describe travel time rather than distance. Here is the central idea. Imagine three warehouse locations between RoboRover’s Dock and its Bay. One route goes from Dock to Junction, then to Ramp, then to Bay. Its edge costs are two meters, three meters, and four meters, for a total of nine meters. A second route goes from Dock to Hall and then to Bay. Its costs are five meters and two meters, for a total of seven meters. The second route is shorter overall, even though it begins with the longer five-meter edge. This is why we judge a route by its complete cost, not just by its cheapest first move. A third route goes from Dock to Junction, then Hall, then Bay. Its costs are two, four, and two meters, totaling eight meters. So, among these three routes, the order is seven meters, eight meters, then nine meters. Route B, through Hall, is the lowest-distance route. Pause and predict: if RoboRover always chooses the cheapest next edge, which route will it begin? And will that necessarily be the cheapest complete route? Take a brief beat. The cheapest immediate edge is Dock to Junction, at two meters. But that choice can lead to the nine-meter or eight-meter routes. The best complete route begins with the more expensive five-meter edge to Hall, and totals only seven meters. Graph search means systematically exploring connected possibilities. It can answer questions such as: Can RoboRover reach the Bay? Which locations are connected to the Dock? Which route has the lowest cost? Or how many movements are needed? Search must also remember what it has already explored. Graphs can contain cycles. Without tracking visited nodes, a search could repeatedly travel from Dock to Junction and back again, treating the loop as endless new progress. A visited set helps prevent that. The Python lab represents the warehouse as a graph. Each node stores its neighboring nodes and the associated edge costs. A recursive search explores possible next steps, keeps a current route, adds each edge cost to a running total, and avoids nodes already visited on that route. After finding the simple routes, the program sorts them by total cost. In this small example, it identifies Dock to Hall to Bay as the best route, with a cost of seven meters. The program also uses assertions to check the expected best route, cost, and number of discovered routes. This is deliberately not Dijkstra’s Algorithm. It explores all simple routes in a small graph and compares them afterward. That approach becomes inefficient as the graph grows, which is why the next class introduces a more systematic lowest-cost search. Now for the most important engineering caveat: a graph is a model, not the warehouse itself. A map may say that an edge exists even when a pallet blocks the corridor. A person may enter an aisle. A wheel may slip, making the robot’s estimated position inaccurate. A corridor’s measured distance may be wrong, or the robot may be unable to turn as sharply as the model assumes. A practical robot separates the graph model, the route search, motion execution, and safety monitoring. Sensors must check whether the planned movement is still safe and available. If the environment disagrees with the graph, RoboRover must stop or revise the plan. There is another modeling tradeoff. A graph with five large nodes is easy to search, but it may hide obstacles inside a corridor. A graph with thousands of tiny nodes can describe space more accurately, but it requires more computation and more map maintenance. Be careful with cost units, too. Adding four meters to three seconds is not meaningful until an engineer defines a common scoring rule. Distance, time, energy, and risk can be combined only after deciding how they should be converted or weighted.
