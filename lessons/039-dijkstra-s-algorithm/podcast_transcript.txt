Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 39, Dijkstra’s Algorithm. In the previous class, we represented a robotics problem as a graph: nodes are places or situations, edges are allowed moves, and a path is a sequence of connected edges. That worked well when every move had the same cost. Real robots are different. A smooth floor may be quick to cross, while carpet, gravel, mud, or a narrow ramp may take more time or battery. Today, we’ll learn Dijkstra’s algorithm, which finds a least-cost path when edges have different, nonnegative costs. The central idea is simple: Dijkstra always selects the not-yet-finalized node with the smallest known total cost from the start. Once selected, that cost is final, provided every edge cost is nonnegative. Think of RoboRover at a starting node called S, trying to reach a goal called G. Its cost wave spreads outward, but not according to geometric distance or the number of moves. It spreads according to accumulated travel cost. A nearby muddy route may be worse than a longer route across smooth floor. Dijkstra keeps two important kinds of information. First, it stores the best cost currently known for reaching each node. Initially, the cost of S is zero, and every other node has infinite cost. Second, it stores each node’s predecessor, or previous node, so the actual route can be reconstructed later. When the algorithm discovers a route to a neighbor, it performs an operation called relaxation. It adds the cost so far to the cost of the next edge. If that candidate total is cheaper than the neighbor’s current known cost, the algorithm updates the neighbor’s cost and predecessor. Here is the worked example. The directed edges and travel times are these: S to A costs two seconds, S to B costs five, A to B costs one, A to C costs four, B to C costs one, B to D costs five, C to D costs one, C to G costs seven, and D to G costs two. Start at S. Its cost is zero, so A becomes two seconds and B becomes five. The cheapest unprocessed node is now A. From A, reaching B costs two plus one, or three seconds. That improves B from five to three. Reaching C through A costs two plus four, or six seconds, so C becomes six. Next, B is the cheapest node at three seconds. Through B, C costs three plus one, or four seconds, improving C from six to four. D becomes three plus five, or eight seconds. Now C is selected at four seconds. Through C, D costs four plus one, or five seconds, improving D. Reaching G directly from C would cost four plus seven, or eleven seconds. D is next at five seconds. From D, G costs five plus two, or seven seconds. G is finalized at seven seconds, and the route is S, A, B, C, D, G. Its total is two plus one plus one plus one plus two: seven seconds. Notice that this route uses five edges. A route with fewer edges is not automatically better. Dijkstra minimizes the total cost defined by the engineering model. If the costs represent time, it minimizes estimated time. If they represent energy, it minimizes estimated energy. Pause and predict: if the edge from C to D becomes muddy and increases from one second to six seconds, will RoboRover still use it? Brief beat. No. The route through B, D, and G costs three plus five plus two, or ten seconds. The route through C directly to G costs four plus seven, or eleven seconds. So the new route is S, A, B, D, G, with a total cost of ten seconds. The algorithm follows a repeating cycle. Initialize the start at zero and all other nodes at infinity. Select the unprocessed node with the smallest known cost. Examine its neighbors, relax any cheaper routes, mark the selected node finalized, and repeat until the goal is finalized or no reachable nodes remain. Finally, follow predecessor links backward from the goal and reverse them to obtain the forward path. Dijkstra requires every edge cost to be nonnegative. That requirement is what makes finalization safe: once the smallest tentative cost is selected, no later route can improve it. Negative edge costs would break that reasoning. For implementation, the Python lab uses an adjacency-list dictionary and Python’s heap queue, or priority queue, module. The distance table stores best-known costs, the predecessor table stores route information, and the priority queue returns the smallest current cost efficiently. The implementation also checks that every named neighbor is present as a graph key, even a goal such as G with no outgoing edges, and rejects negative edge costs. One practical detail is the stale-entry check. A node can be placed in the priority queue more than once. If a cheaper route is found later, an older, more expensive entry may still be waiting. The program ignores that old entry rather than processing it again. The lab also tests three situations: the normal map, the muddy connection, and an unreachable goal. For an unreachable goal, it returns an empty path and infinite cost, meaning no finite route exists in the supplied graph. With an adjacency list and a binary heap, the typical time complexity is order of V plus E, multiplied by the logarithm of V, and the space complexity is order of V plus E, where V is the number of nodes and E is the number of directed edges. Now for the most important engineering caveat: Dijkstra is only as good as the graph and cost model supplied to it. A calculated cost of seven seconds is an estimate, not a promise. Real travel can be affected by wheel slip, acceleration, braking, turning, battery state, sensor delay, load, or obstacles missing from the map. The simple model also assumes that edge costs add independently.
