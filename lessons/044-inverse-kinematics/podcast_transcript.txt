Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 44: Inverse Kinematics. Last class, RoboRover learned forward kinematics: given the joint angles of a two-link arm, it calculated where the tip would go. Today we reverse that process. Given a desired tip position, what joint angles will place the arm there? That problem is inverse kinematics, often shortened to IK. Imagine RoboRover’s inspection arm. Its shoulder rotates the first link, and its elbow rotates the second. A camera at the tip needs to reach a marked point on a workbench. Our task is to calculate the shoulder angle and elbow angle that make that happen. For a two-dimensional arm, let the link lengths be L one and L two. Let the joint angles be theta one and theta two, and let the target position be x, y. Forward kinematics uses the angles to calculate the target. Inverse kinematics uses the target to calculate the angles. There is an important surprise: one target can often have two valid arm configurations. The elbow can lie on either side of the straight line from the shoulder to the target. These are commonly called elbow-up and elbow-down. Pause and predict: for a target directly to the right of the shoulder, can the arm usually reach it with the elbow above the line or below the line? Brief beat. For a reachable interior target, it can usually do both. The elbow can be above the shoulder-to-target line or below it. At the outer edge of the workspace, the two configurations merge into one fully stretched arm. At the inner edge, they merge into one fully folded configuration when the links have unequal lengths. There is one special equal-link case. If both links have the same length and the target is exactly at the shoulder, the arm can fold back onto itself in infinitely many physical orientations. The shoulder angle is not uniquely defined. Let’s see the geometry. First, calculate the straight-line distance from the shoulder to the target. In plain language, that distance is the square root of x squared plus y squared. Call it r. The target is reachable only if r is at least the absolute difference between the two link lengths, and no greater than their sum. The outer limit is easy to picture: stretch both links straight. The inner limit describes the central unreachable region for unequal links. The elbow angle comes from the law of cosines. The cosine of theta two is the target distance squared, minus the first link length squared, minus the second link length squared, all divided by twice the product of the link lengths. Taking inverse cosine gives one branch. Negating that elbow angle gives the other geometric branch. Then the shoulder angle is found using atan2, which is a safer inverse-tangent function because it considers the signs of both coordinates and identifies the correct quadrant. Here is the worked example. RoboRover has a first link of zero point six metres and a second link of zero point four metres. The target is at zero point seven metres horizontally and zero point three metres vertically. The squared target distance is zero point seven squared plus zero point three squared, which is zero point five eight square metres. Substituting into the law-of-cosines expression gives a cosine value of zero point one two five. The positive elbow branch is approximately eighty-two point eight degrees. The corresponding shoulder angle is approximately negative eight point two degrees. With the convention used here, that is the elbow-down configuration. Its elbow lies below the shoulder-to-target line. Negating the elbow angle gives negative eighty-two point eight degrees. The corresponding shoulder angle is approximately fifty-four point six degrees. That is the elbow-up configuration, with the elbow above the line. Both configurations place the tip at approximately zero point seven, zero point three metres. Their elbow positions are different, but their final camera positions are the same. Be careful with the labels. The sign of theta two does not universally define elbow-up or elbow-down. Different coordinate systems and angle conventions can reverse those names. Classify the branch from the elbow’s actual geometric position. The Python lab turns this calculation into an experiment. You provide the link lengths and target coordinates, and the program calculates both candidate solutions. It sends each solution back through forward kinematics to verify that the reconstructed tip matches the target. It also plots the arm, the maximum-reach circle, and, for unequal links, the inner exclusion boundary. The program checks for boundary cases, removes duplicate geometric solutions when the branches coincide, and identifies branches using the elbow’s position rather than angle sign alone. Try changing the target to zero point two, zero point nine metres. Its distance from the shoulder is approximately zero point nine two two metres. Since that is between the inner radius of zero point two metres and the maximum reach of one metre, it is an interior reachable target and should produce two configurations. Now try one point one, zero metres. That target is one point one metres away, but this arm can reach only one metre. There is no real inverse-kinematics solution, and the program should report that the target is outside maximum reach. The most important engineering caveat is this: a mathematically valid solution is not automatically a usable motor command. A real robot must check joint limits, obstacles, self-collisions, cable routing, motor torque, speed, acceleration, and coordinate-frame conversions. Mathematical angles may be measured from a horizontal axis, while an encoder’s zero position may be defined somewhere else. Real hardware also introduces flexible links, inaccurate measurements, gear backlash, encoder offsets, load-induced bending, rounding, and sensor noise. Even correct equations may not place the physical tip exactly where expected. The fully stretched and fully folded configurations are also singular. Near these postures, the arm loses useful movement freedom in some directions, and a small Cartesian motion may require unusually large joint changes. The equal-link shoulder case is orientation-degenerate because the target position does not determine a unique shoulder direction.
