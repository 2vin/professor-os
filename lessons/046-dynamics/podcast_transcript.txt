Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 46: Dynamics. In the previous class, RoboRover learned how a gripper holds, lifts, and releases an object. Today, we ask a different question: how does carrying that object change the way the whole robot moves? Dynamics is the study of how forces and moments produce motion. Translational dynamics describes motion of the robot’s center of mass from place to place. Rotational dynamics describes changes in orientation caused by torque, or moment. Our main focus today is one-dimensional translational motion, with a few connections to turning, payload height, and tipping. The central equation is Newton’s second law: net force equals mass times acceleration. In plain language, acceleration equals net force divided by mass. Mass measures resistance to changes in motion. A nearly empty shopping cart starts easily. A cart full of books needs more force to produce the same change in speed. A robot carrying a toolbox behaves in the same way. Before we continue, pause and predict: if RoboRover carries twice as much mass while its motors produce the same net force, will its acceleration double, stay the same, or become smaller? Take a brief beat and make your choice. The answer is: its acceleration becomes smaller. Since acceleration equals net force divided by mass, doubling the mass while keeping force unchanged reduces acceleration by half. To apply this correctly, distinguish applied force from net force. RoboRover experiences several forces at once. The wheels push backward on the ground, and the ground pushes forward on the wheels. For the complete rover-and-toolbox system, the forward force in the motion equation is the external contact force of the ground on the wheels. The wheels’ backward force on the ground is the equal-and-opposite interaction, not the forward force acting on the robot. Other external forces include gravity, the normal force from the ground, and rolling resistance. On level ground, gravity and the normal force often approximately cancel vertically. In the horizontal direction, a useful simplified model is net force equals drive force minus resistance force. When drawing a free-body diagram, first select the system. For the complete rover and toolbox, wheel-ground traction, gravity, the ground’s normal force, and rolling resistance are external forces. Motor torque inside the drivetrain and gripper contact with the toolbox are internal interactions within that selected system, so they are not drawn as separate external forces. If forward force exceeds opposing force, RoboRover accelerates forward. If the forces balance, acceleration is zero. The rover may be stopped, or it may already be moving at constant velocity. Zero net force does not necessarily mean zero velocity. Here is the worked example. RoboRover has a mass of 12 kilograms, including its battery and empty gripper. It picks up a 3-kilogram toolbox, so the combined mass is 15 kilograms. Suppose the external forward wheel-ground drive force is 30 newtons, and rolling resistance opposes motion with 6 newtons. The net force is therefore 30 minus 6, or 24 newtons. Now divide net force by mass. Twenty-four newtons divided by 15 kilograms gives an acceleration of 1.6 meters per second squared forward. This does not mean the rover instantly reaches 1.6 meters per second. It means that, while the model remains valid, its velocity changes by 1.6 meters per second each second. If it starts from rest and the force stays constant for 2 seconds, its ideal final velocity is 3.2 meters per second. Without the toolbox, the mass is 12 kilograms. With the same 24-newton net force, acceleration is 2.0 meters per second squared. The payload reduces acceleration from 2.0 to 1.6 meters per second squared. That example illustrates the main engineering relationship: same net force plus more mass equals less acceleration. There are several important distinctions here. Translational acceleration changes the velocity of the center of mass. Yaw rate is how quickly the robot’s heading changes. Angular acceleration is how quickly that yaw rate changes. A lateral net force can produce lateral acceleration and contribute to path curvature, but it does not automatically change the robot’s heading. Heading change requires angular velocity and appropriate torque, wheel-force distribution, steering geometry, or another kinematic constraint. The most important engineering caveat is that force is not always constant in a real robot. Motor force can decrease as speed rises. Tires can slip if the requested force exceeds available tire-ground friction. Battery voltage, gearing, software limits, floor conditions, rolling resistance, air drag, slopes, wheel torque, and payload placement can all matter. The simple equation remains valuable because it identifies the dominant relationship among force, mass, and acceleration. Engineers then measure where the simple model stops matching the physical robot. The Python lab explores this relationship conceptually. It simulates one-dimensional motion for a light rover and a loaded rover using small time steps. At each step, it calculates acceleration from net force and mass, updates velocity, and then updates position using the new velocity. That approach is called semi-implicit Euler integration. The lab compares numerical position with the exact analytic position for constant acceleration. For the 12-kilogram rover, the analytic position after 2 seconds is 4.00 meters. With a time step of 0.01 seconds, the semi-implicit simulation gives 4.02 meters. That small difference is a numerical-method effect, not extra physical force. Reducing the time step reduces this particular position difference, while the analytic result remains unchanged. The lab also lets you change mass, force, and time step. For example, doubling both force and mass leaves acceleration unchanged, because both the numerator and denominator double. Changing only the mass or only the force changes acceleration. This is a practical way to connect equations with plotted velocity and position curves. Payload mass is not the only concern. Payload location matters too. A higher or offset payload can increase moments during braking or turning and can increase tipping risk. Mass changes translational inertia, while payload geometry changes force distribution and rotational behavior.
