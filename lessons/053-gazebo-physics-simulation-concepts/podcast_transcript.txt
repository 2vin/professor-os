Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 53, Gazebo and Physics Simulation Concepts. In the previous class, we used robot simulation as a way to test RoboRover without risking physical hardware. Today, we make that idea more concrete by examining three parts of a physics simulator: worlds, collisions, and sensors. A simulated world is the complete virtual environment. It can contain a ground plane, walls, ramps, boxes, lights, gravity, movable objects, robots, and sensors. It also includes physical properties such as mass and friction, along with simulated time. The robot model may contain a body, wheels, joints, motors, and sensors. A physics engine uses these models and rules to predict motion and contact. It can calculate whether gravity makes an object fall, whether a wheel pushes against the ground, whether two objects touch, and how friction affects sliding. A useful loop is: the controller sends a command, the simulator calculates motion, simulated sensors produce observations, and the controller uses those observations to choose what to do next. Remember, the simulated position is not automatically the robot’s real position. It is a value produced by a model. If the model is inaccurate, virtual behavior and physical behavior can differ. One especially important distinction is between visual geometry, collision geometry, and sensor geometry. Visual geometry is what a person sees. Collision geometry is what the simulator uses to calculate contact and overlap. Sensor geometry, such as a ray from a range sensor, is what the simulated device uses to measure the world. These representations do not have to be identical. RoboRover might look like a detailed vehicle with wheels and a camera, while its collision model is only a simple box or cylinder. Simplifying collision geometry can reduce computation while preserving the behavior that matters for an experiment. Collision detection asks, “Are these shapes touching or overlapping?” Collision response asks, “What should happen because they are touching?” A full physics engine might calculate contact locations, surface directions, penetration depth, forces, and corrections that separate objects. Common collision shapes include spheres, boxes, cylinders, and capsules. A triangle mesh can represent a more complex surface, but it may require more computation and careful settings. Sensors provide a limited view of the world. A simulated range sensor may measure distance. A camera may produce an image. An inertial sensor may report acceleration and angular velocity. Wheel encoders may report rotation, and a contact sensor may report whether a bumper is pressed. A simulated sensor can be ideal, or it can include noise, limited range, delay, a blind spot, a restricted field of view, missed detections, or quantization. So a sensor reading is not the world itself. It is one particular measurement from one location and direction. Let’s work through a simple circular collision model. Suppose RoboRover’s center is at one point, and an obstacle’s center is six-tenths of a metre away. RoboRover has a radius of twenty centimetres, and the obstacle has a radius of thirty centimetres. The combined radius is fifty centimetres. Because the centre distance, sixty centimetres, is greater than the combined radius, fifty centimetres, the circles are separated by ten centimetres. This model predicts no contact. If RoboRover moves another ten centimetres toward the obstacle, the centre distance becomes fifty centimetres. The circles just touch. A general contact test reports contact when the distance is less than or equal to the sum of the radii. The small Python lab uses a slightly different policy for motion. It blocks overlap, but accepts exact tangency. That means it uses a strict less-than comparison when deciding whether a proposed position should be rejected. This is a deliberate teaching choice, not a universal simulator rule. Pause and predict: if RoboRover’s sensor is mounted conceptually at its centre and the rover stops one radius from the obstacle’s face, will the sensor reading be zero metres, one rover radius, or the full obstacle distance? Take a moment. The answer is one rover radius. The sensor measures from the rover’s centre to the obstacle’s face, while the collision rule protects the rover’s outer boundary. So if the rover radius is twenty-five centimetres, the centre can be twenty-five centimetres from the face while the rover itself is exactly touching it. Here is the larger worked example. A rectangular obstacle begins at an x-coordinate of four metres. RoboRover has a radius of zero point two five metres, starts at x equals one metre and y equals three metres, and drives directly east at zero point five five metres per second. The simulator advances by zero point two seconds at a time. The movement per step is speed multiplied by time: zero point five five times zero point two, which equals zero point one one metres. The rover continues proposing positions until the next position would overlap the obstacle. The closest accepted centre position is four metres minus zero point two five metres, or three point seven five metres. At that point, the rover is tangent to the obstacle but does not overlap under the lab’s policy. The ideal forward sensor measures from the rover’s centre to the obstacle’s front face. Its reading is four minus three point seven five, which equals zero point two five metres. The Python program demonstrates this behavior conceptually. It updates the rover in fixed time steps, checks each proposed position against an axis-aligned rectangle, rejects overlapping moves, simulates an ideal forward range sensor, records the path, and displays the result with Matplotlib. Assertions verify the expected final position, accepted and blocked moves, and sensor reading. It is important to understand what this program does not do. It is a discrete, kinematic collision proxy, not a Gazebo implementation and not a full physics engine. It does not solve forces, friction, wheel rotation, body tilt, angular motion, contact manifolds, or continuous-time physics. That limitation points to the most important engineering caveat: simulation is useful, but it is an approximation.
