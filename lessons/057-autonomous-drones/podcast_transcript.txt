Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 57, Autonomous Drones. In the previous class, RoboRover learned imitation learning: a policy that maps observations to actions resembling those demonstrated by a teacher. Today, RoboRover gains a new body, a small autonomous quadrotor scout. That scout must stay airborne, estimate its motion, and choose task-level actions without a human continuously commanding every motor. The central idea is that learned behavior and physical control solve different parts of the problem. Imitation learning can suggest what action seems appropriate. Sensors estimate what the drone is actually doing. Feedback control changes actuator commands to reduce the difference between the desired and measured state. Autonomy concerns who or what selects actions toward the task. A drone has six degrees of freedom, or six DoF. It can translate along three axes and rotate around three axes. The rotations are roll, pitch, and yaw. Together, position and orientation describe the drone’s pose. Six degrees of freedom do not mean six motors. A quadrotor has four motor inputs. Coordinated changes in those inputs create total thrust and three principal body torques. The vehicle tilts to redirect thrust before producing horizontal acceleration, so its motion is coordinated rather than controlled as six completely independent channels. It is important to distinguish two coordinate frames. The world frame is fixed to the room, launch site, or map. The body frame moves and rotates with the drone. Body x commonly points forward, body y points sideways, and body z follows the convention chosen for the model. In this lesson, body z points upward along the thrust direction. That is a teaching convention, not a universal one. Many flight-control texts define body z downward, so the signs in thrust and attitude equations can change. Always state which frame and sign convention you are using. Suppose the drone pitches forward to move forward. Its thrust axis tilts, so total thrust now has a horizontal component. But the vertical component becomes smaller. To maintain altitude, the controller must increase total thrust to compensate. Here is a memorable calculation. RoboRover’s aerial scout has a mass of 1.20 kilograms. In ideal level hover, thrust balances weight. Weight is mass times gravity, so the required total thrust is 1.20 times 9.81, or 11.772 newtons. If four rotors share the load equally, each contributes about 2.943 newtons. Now imagine the drone tilts by 10 degrees while maintaining altitude. Only the vertical part of thrust supports the weight. The total thrust must therefore be weight divided by the cosine of 10 degrees, which is approximately 11.954 newtons. The horizontal component is about 2.076 newtons. Dividing that force by the 1.20-kilogram mass gives a horizontal acceleration of about 1.730 meters per second squared, ignoring drag. The important lesson is that thrust is a force, not an acceleration. Acceleration comes from force divided by mass. And when the drone tilts, thrust is shared between vertical support and horizontal motion. Pause and predict: if a drone’s altitude sensor says it is below its target, should the controller generally increase or decrease thrust? Brief beat. It should increase thrust. If the desired altitude is two meters and the measured altitude is 1.85 meters, the altitude error is 0.15 meters. With a proportional gain of 18 newtons per meter, the correction is 2.7 newtons. Adding that to the 11.772-newton hover thrust gives a simplified command of 14.472 newtons. This is feedback control. The controller repeatedly chooses a desired state, measures or estimates the current state, compares the two, and applies an action that reduces the difference. The drone does not measure perfect truth. An inertial measurement unit contains accelerometers and gyroscopes, but those sensors have noise and bias. A barometer estimates altitude from pressure. A downward range sensor may measure slant distance rather than true vertical altitude when the vehicle is tilted. Cameras can struggle in darkness or in areas without visual texture. Measurements may also be delayed. That is the most important engineering caveat: the controller acts on measurements and estimates, not on perfect knowledge of the state. Noise can cause jitter. Bias can create persistent error. Delay can make the controller respond to an earlier condition. Actuator limits can prevent the requested thrust from being produced. A mathematically reasonable command may still fail when the hardware saturates. The Python lab demonstrates these ideas with a deliberately simple vertical model. It tracks true altitude and vertical velocity, creates a noisy altitude measurement, and uses a proportional correction plus a velocity-dependent damping term. It also limits thrust to a specified range. The simulation is not a flight simulator, and because it supplies true simulated velocity directly, it is PD-like rather than a complete real-world proportional-derivative controller. When you run it, the drone should rise gradually toward two meters rather than teleporting there. The measured altitude should jitter around the true altitude. Initial thrust should be above hover thrust, then move closer to hover as the error shrinks. Increasing proportional gain makes the response more aggressive and may increase overshoot. Removing the damping term generally increases overshoot or oscillation. Increasing sensor noise makes the measurement and thrust traces rougher. Automation, feedback control, and autonomy should also remain separate. A fixed takeoff sequence can be automated. A teleoperated drone can use feedback control. An autonomous system uses software to select task-level actions, such as choosing inspection waypoints, while respecting geofences, battery limits, and altitude limits. A higher-level policy may decide where to go, while lower-level control loops determine how to produce the required motion safely. In a real drone, these layers form a stack. A task or human command becomes a desired position or velocity. An outer position or altitude loop converts that request into desired attitude and thrust. An inner attitude and rate loop stabilizes the aircraft. State estimation combines sensor information, and actuator mixing converts total thrust and torque requests into individual motor commands.
