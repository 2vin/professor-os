Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 1, “What Is a Robot?” We’re beginning with a question that sounds simple but has surprising depth: what makes a machine a robot? Meet RoboRover, our course companion. RoboRover is a small wheeled machine with motors, a battery, sensors, and a computer. Today, we will not build advanced navigation or control systems. Instead, we’ll learn how to describe RoboRover accurately. There is no single universally accepted boundary for the word robot. Engineers, industries, museums, and textbooks may use the term somewhat differently. So this course uses a clear working description. For this course, a robot is a physical machine commonly treated as a robot in engineering practice, whose controlled actuators perform a physical task. Its immediate actions may be selected by a human operator, a preprogrammed controller, or autonomous software. This is a teaching convention, not a universal necessary-and-sufficient test. The key point is that robot identity and decision authority are separate questions. A robot does not need to be fully autonomous. Here’s a quick prediction. A human uses a handheld controller to drive RoboRover across a room. Is RoboRover a robot? The answer is yes, under our course convention. A human selects the immediate motion, but RoboRover is still a physical machine whose controlled actuators perform a physical task. Now consider a thermostat. It measures room temperature and switches a heater on or off. Is it normally called a robot? No, not normally, although it clearly uses feedback control. These examples separate two questions: is this physical system commonly treated as a robot performing a physical task through controlled actuators, and who or what selects the immediate action? To analyze a robotic system, use three layers. First, the machine: the physical object, including the chassis, wheels, motors, battery, sensors, and computer. Second, the decision authority: the person, stored controller, or software that selects what the machine should do next. Third, the environment: relevant parts of the world outside the machine that can affect it or be affected by it. For RoboRover, that could include the floor, walls, objects, lighting, people, temperature, and air. The boundary between machine and environment is a modeling boundary. It may not be a visible line. It simply tells us which components we are analyzing as the robot and which outside conditions belong to the environment. A camera mounted on RoboRover is a robot component. A remote operator is outside the robot, even though the operator’s commands influence it. Wireless communication or power can cross the boundary without making the transmitter or power source part of the rover. Forces can cross it too: the wheels push against the floor, and a rover may contact a wall. Those outside forces do not automatically become robot components. An actuator is a device that creates physical action, such as a motor turning a wheel or a gripper closing. A calculator performs computation, but it does not usually perform a physical task through controlled actuators. A motorized robotic arm does. Now freeze RoboRover’s hardware and change only who chooses the next command. In teleoperation, a human presses forward, left, or stop. In preprogrammed operation, a stored sequence selects the actions. In autonomous operation, software selects task-level actions from available information. The physical machine remains the same. The control mode changes, but RoboRover’s robot identity does not automatically disappear. Autonomy means that software selects task-level actions without a human choosing every immediate motion. It does not mean consciousness, unlimited independence, or perfect intelligence. An autonomous vacuum may still have a human-set schedule and charging station. Its autonomy concerns how it chooses actions during operation. Now let’s preview feedback control. Broadly, feedback control means that a measured state or output influences a current or future control action in relation to a desired behavior or state. A thermostat gives a simple example. The desired room temperature is the setpoint. The measured room temperature comes from a sensor. If the measured temperature is below the setpoint, heating may turn on; if it is above the setpoint, heating may turn off. This is feedback even though the rule is simple and uses thresholds. Feedback does not have to be continuous or proportional. In this course, we’ll use the term sustained feedback regulation when we specifically mean repeated measurement and correction over time. A one-shot sensor event that starts a fixed five-second motor sequence is not sustained feedback regulation if later measurements do not alter that sequence. Here is a worked example. Suppose RoboRover travels at a constant speed of zero point two metres per second for fifteen seconds. For constant speed, distance equals speed multiplied by time. So the modeled distance is zero point two metres per second times fifteen seconds, which equals three point zero metres. The seconds cancel, leaving metres. This means RoboRover’s controlled actuators have produced physical motion through a three-metre distance. That is a physical task, even if a human selected every movement. Now compare the decision authority. A person might hold the forward button for fifteen seconds: that is teleoperation. A stored controller might say, “Drive forward for fifteen seconds”: that is preprogrammed task execution. Or software might measure position and decide when to stop near three metres: that uses task-level autonomy. The motion model is similar, but the decision authority differs. The most important engineering caveat is that the command “zero point two metres per second” may not produce exactly that speed. The two wheels may differ slightly. The floor may be slippery. Battery voltage may fall. Wheel slip may allow the motors to turn without producing the expected movement. Real robots therefore need measurements, testing, and calibration. A controller that assumes perfect motion may stop too early, too late, or at an angle. The Python lab gives you a small simulation of this idea. It uses the same RoboRover model in teleoperated, preprogrammed, and autonomous modes. A command means drive forward, stop, or drive backward.
