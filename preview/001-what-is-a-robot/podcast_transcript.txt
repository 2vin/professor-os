Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 1, “What Is a Robot?” Welcome to the beginning of our robotics course. Our recurring example is RoboRover, a small wheeled machine with sensors, a computer, motors, and a battery. Today we are building vocabulary that we will use throughout the course: robot identity, decision authority, autonomy, the boundary between a machine and its environment, and an introductory idea of feedback control. Let’s start with a question. If a machine has wheels, is it automatically a robot? No. Wheels alone do not determine robot identity. A wheelbarrow has wheels, but people usually do not call it a robot. A motorized toy may move, but whether we call it a robot depends on its physical task, its controlled actuators, and the engineering context. Now a second question: if a human controls a machine, can it still be a robot? Yes. A teleoperated rover is commonly called a robot even when a human chooses its immediate motion. These questions reveal the central idea for this class: robot identity and decision authority are separate. For this course, we use this working description. A robot is a physical machine commonly treated as a robot in engineering practice, whose controlled actuators perform a physical task. Its immediate actions may be selected by a human operator, a preprogrammed controller, or autonomous software. This is a teaching description, not a universal necessary-and-sufficient test. So RoboRover remains the same physical robot in three situations. A human might drive it with a controller. A stored program might tell it to move forward, stop, and turn. Or autonomous software might select task-level actions using available information. The hardware can remain nearly identical. What changes is the source of the immediate motion command. An actuator is a device that produces controlled physical movement or force. A motor turning a wheel is an actuator. So is a joint motor lifting an arm, or a gripper motor closing fingers. A robot performs a physical task such as driving, lifting, sorting, carrying, painting, inspecting, or manipulating an object. Now let’s draw a practical boundary. RoboRover’s chassis, wheels, motors, battery, computer, and onboard sensors are part of the machine. The floor, walls, objects, people, lighting, and surrounding air are part of the environment. The sensors are physically on the rover, but they measure the environment. This boundary is an engineering choice that should be stated clearly. For example, a charging station might be outside the rover in one analysis, but part of a larger robot system in another. A useful beginner approach is to ask what moves or supplies power, what measures or computes inside the machine, and what exists outside and is affected or measured. Autonomy is another term that needs precision. In common robotics usage, autonomy means software selects actions at a task level without a human choosing every immediate command. An autonomous vacuum may decide when to move, stop, or change direction during cleaning. That does not mean the vacuum is independent of all humans. People may still choose its schedule, charge it, define its operating area, or maintain it. Autonomy is not the same as intelligence in every situation, freedom from maintenance, or having no human influence. Autonomy and feedback are also separate concepts. Software can autonomously choose a task action while a lower-level controller uses feedback to regulate a motor. Feedback control means that a measured state or output influences a current or future control action in relation to a desired state or behavior. Consider a thermostat. The desired temperature is 20 degrees Celsius. The measured room temperature is 18 degrees, so the controller turns heating on. A later measurement shows that the room has reached the desired range, and the controller changes the heating action. The rule may have been fixed in advance, but the action responds to measurement. Feedback does not have to be continuous or smoothly proportional. A rule such as “turn the fan on when the temperature reaches 24 degrees” also responds to a measured state. But one sensor event does not necessarily create sustained feedback regulation. If RoboRover detects a marker once and then drives for exactly five seconds without using later measurements to adjust its action, that timed motion is not being repeatedly regulated by the measured state. Here is our worked example. RoboRover travels in a straight line at a constant average speed. Distance equals average speed multiplied by elapsed time. If its average speed is zero point six meters per second and it drives for eight seconds, the distance is zero point six times eight, or 4.8 meters. The units confirm the result: meters per second multiplied by seconds gives meters. Pause and predict: does this calculation tell us whether the rover is autonomous? …The answer is no. It describes motion, not decision authority. A human, a timer, or autonomous software could all command the same eight-second drive. In the hallway test, a human operator presses the forward button for eight seconds. The rover’s measured average speed is zero point six meters per second, so the approximate distance is 4.8 meters. The rover is still a robot even though the human selected the immediate action. Now replace the button press with a stored instruction: drive forward for eight seconds, then stop. The hardware and approximate distance may remain the same, but the decision authority changes from human operator to preprogrammed controller. Finally, suppose software selects “drive forward” because its task is to approach a visible work area. The decision authority is now autonomous software. The most important engineering caveat is that the distance calculation is a model, not a guarantee of exact placement. It assumes a known average speed and simplified motion. Real motors need time to accelerate. Floors may be uneven. Wheels can slip, battery voltage can change, and one wheel may have less grip than another, causing the rover to curve. Engineers check calibration, measure behavior, account for actuator limits, and use feedback when appropriate.
