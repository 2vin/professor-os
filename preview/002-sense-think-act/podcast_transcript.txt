Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 2, “Sense → Think → Act.” In our first class, we defined a robot as a physical machine whose controlled actuators perform a physical task. We also separated robot identity from decision authority. A human may control a teleoperated rover, while software may control an autonomous vacuum. Both can be robots. Today, we open the robot’s body and brain to study a repeating pattern behind many robotic systems: sense, think, act. Our example is RoboRover, a small wheeled robot trying to maintain a safe distance from a wall. RoboRover has a forward-facing distance sensor, two powered wheels, a battery, and a small computer. Its target is to stop about 0.50 metres from the wall. First, it senses. The distance sensor estimates the rover’s separation from the wall. Then it thinks. The controller compares that measurement with the desired distance and selects a command. Finally, it acts. The motors turn the wheels forward, backward, or not at all. Then the rover senses again. That repeated measurement is essential. Without measuring again, RoboRover cannot tell whether its previous action worked. This is a feedback control loop. In feedback control, a measured state or output influences a current or future action in relation to a desired state. The robot acts, measures the result, and uses the result to decide what happens next. Let’s define the three main parts. A sensor measures a property of the robot or its environment and produces information for the controller. A distance sensor measures separation. A wheel encoder measures wheel rotation. A camera measures patterns of light. A bumper switch reports contact. But a sensor does not automatically understand the world. It produces a signal or value. Electronics and software interpret that value. A controller receives measurements and selects commands. For RoboRover, a simple threshold controller might use three rules: if the wall is farther than the target zone, drive forward; if the wall is closer than the target zone, drive backward; otherwise, stop. An actuator converts a command into physical action. Motors turn wheels, servos move joints, pumps move liquid, and grippers close fingers. A controller may request a particular motor speed, but the physical result can differ because of friction, battery voltage, load, or mechanical wear. Here is a worked example. RoboRover wants to be 0.50 metres from the wall. Its sensor reads 0.68 metres. We define distance error as measured distance minus target distance. So the error is 0.68 metres minus 0.50 metres, which equals positive 0.18 metres. The positive sign means the rover is too far from the wall. Since it is facing the wall, the controller commands forward motion. Later, the sensor reads 0.52 metres. The error is 0.52 minus 0.50, or positive 0.02 metres. Suppose the acceptable error magnitude is 0.04 metres. Because 0.02 is within that acceptable zone, the controller commands stop. Notice the essential pattern: the target stayed the same, the measurement changed, and the controller selected a different actuator command because of that measurement. Pause and predict. RoboRover starts 1.20 metres from the wall, with a target of 0.50 metres. What should its first movement command be: forward, backward, or stop? Brief beat. The answer is forward, because the measured distance is greater than the target plus the deadband, or acceptable zone. As RoboRover approaches, its distance should generally decrease. Once it enters the deadband, it should usually stop rather than drive continuously. The most important engineering caveat is that real robots do not receive perfect information or produce perfect motion. Sensor noise can make measurements fluctuate even when the rover is still. Calibration may be imperfect, so a reported 0.50 metres may not be the true distance. Delay means the controller may be acting on an old measurement. Motors have limits, wheels can slip, and unequal motors can make the rover curve. A deadband helps with noise. Instead of changing commands for every tiny difference, the controller accepts a range around the target. For example, it might stop whenever the rover is within 0.04 metres of the desired distance. This reduces unnecessary forward-and-backward switching. But it does not eliminate every problem. Delay can still cause overshoot, and fixed-speed motion may carry the rover past the target before the next correction takes effect. A simple travel estimate also shows why units matter. If RoboRover must travel 0.32 metres at a constant speed of 0.20 metres per second, the estimated time is distance divided by speed: 0.32 divided by 0.20, or 1.6 seconds. That estimate assumes perfect measurement, immediate motor response, constant speed, and no slipping. Real robots rarely satisfy all of those assumptions. In the Python lab, you will explore this loop through a small simulation rather than reading or writing a complete motor controller. The simulated rover begins 1.20 metres from the wall and tries to maintain 0.50 metres. The program adds small random noise to the sensor reading, calculates signed error, chooses forward, backward, or stop using a threshold rule, and updates the rover’s true distance in time steps. It then plots distance over time. The simulation separates true distance from measured distance. That distinction is important: a noisy sensor report is not necessarily the physical truth. The lab also uses assertions to check structural and safety conditions while the program runs. It is a teaching model, not a full representation of a real rover. It ignores acceleration, wheel slip, sensor delay, and the time a motor needs to start or stop. Feedback control is not the same as autonomy. A robot can follow a preprogrammed task while its motors use feedback. A sensor trigger that starts a fixed sequence is not necessarily sustained feedback regulation. Sustained feedback means repeated measurement and correction over time, with later measurements influencing later actions. Real robots often contain several nested Sense → Think → Act loops. A high-level program may decide where to go. A distance controller may keep the rover away from obstacles. Wheel-speed controllers may use encoder feedback.
