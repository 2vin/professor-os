Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 4, Coordinates: How Robots Describe Space. In our first classes, we defined robots, introduced RoboRover’s sensors, actuators, and controller, and wrote a simple Python program that could receive information and choose an action. But a robot needs more than the instruction, “Move over there.” Where is “there”? How far away is it? Is the target in front of RoboRover, behind it, or to one side? Robots answer these questions using coordinates: numbers that describe location. Today, we’ll give RoboRover a simple map, a flat x-y plane. A coordinate map has two rulers crossing at a special point called the origin. The origin is written as zero, zero. The horizontal ruler is the x-axis. Positive x points right, and negative x points left. The vertical ruler is the y-axis. Positive y points upward, and negative y points downward. A location is written as an ordered pair: x, y. The order matters. The first number is the horizontal coordinate, and the second is the vertical coordinate. So the point four, two is not the same as the point two, four. Pause and predict: If RoboRover is at location three, four, which number tells us its horizontal position: three or four? Brief beat. The answer is three. The first number is x, the horizontal position. The second number, four, is y, the vertical position. For this lesson, we’ll treat RoboRover as a point on the map. In a real physical system, that point might represent the rover’s centre, a defined reference point, or a sensor location. The system has to specify which one is being modelled. We also need a scale. If one coordinate unit represents one metre, then four, two means four metres right and two metres up from the origin. If one unit represents ten centimetres, those same numbers mean fifty centimetres right and twenty centimetres up. The numbers alone are not enough. Software must know the units, orientation, and origin. Now let’s connect coordinates to distance. For a point x, y, the straight-line distance from the origin is the square root of x squared plus y squared. This is the Pythagorean theorem applied to a coordinate map. For two points, A and B, the distance is the square root of the horizontal difference squared plus the vertical difference squared. In plain language, subtract the x-coordinates, subtract the y-coordinates, square both differences, add them, and take the square root. The differences are often called delta x and delta y. Delta means change in, or difference. These differences are signed, so a negative result records direction. For example, a horizontal difference of negative three means the target is three units to the left. The separation itself is the nonnegative magnitude, three units. Squaring the differences in the distance formula ensures that direction does not change the final distance. Here’s a worked example. RoboRover is at one point two metres, negative zero point five metres. A charging station is at four point two metres, one point five metres. First, the horizontal difference is four point two minus one point two, which equals three metres. The vertical difference is one point five minus negative zero point five, which equals two metres. The parentheses matter here: subtracting a negative value increases the result. The direct distance is therefore the square root of three squared plus two squared. That is the square root of nine plus four, or the square root of thirteen, approximately three point six one metres. So the charging station is about three point six one metres away in a straight line on the map. That last phrase is the most important engineering caveat in this lesson. This is Euclidean straight-line distance on a flat Cartesian map. It is not necessarily the distance RoboRover can drive. Walls, shelves, safety zones, or other obstacles may force the rover to take a longer route. Finding a feasible route is a later path-planning problem. Let’s compare two targets. RoboRover is at negative one, two metres. The blue marker is at two, six metres. The yellow marker is at three, three metres. For the blue marker, the horizontal difference is three and the vertical difference is four. Its distance is the square root of three squared plus four squared: the square root of twenty-five, or five metres. For the yellow marker, the horizontal difference is four and the vertical difference is one. Its distance is the square root of four squared plus one squared: the square root of seventeen, approximately four point one two metres. Therefore, the yellow marker is closer. We cannot decide that by looking at only the x-coordinate or only the y-coordinate. Distance depends on both coordinates together. The Python lab turns these ideas into a small computational experiment. It stores RoboRover and several targets as coordinate pairs, calculates each target’s Euclidean distance, checks expected results with assertions, and plots the points on a map using the matplotlib library. For the chosen example, RoboRover is at one, negative one. Targets A and C are each exactly five units away. Target B is closest, at the square root of eight, approximately two point eight two eight units. Target D is farthest, at ten units. The plot also draws a five-unit circle centred on RoboRover. A and C lie on that circle, showing that they are equally far away even though they are in different directions. The graph should use equal horizontal and vertical scaling. Otherwise, a circle can look stretched into an ellipse and mislead your eyes. The lab coordinates are map values supplied by the program. They are not automatically measurements from a physical robot. In practice, sensors or localization software estimate position, and those estimates can contain error. That leads to another important engineering habit: always ask, “Relative to which origin, in which direction, and using what units?” A fixed room or map is a simplified world frame. A coordinate system attached to the moving robot is a body frame.
