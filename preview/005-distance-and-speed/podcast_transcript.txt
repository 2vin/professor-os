Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 5: Distance and Speed. In the previous class, RoboRover learned to describe where it is using coordinates. A position such as two meters in x and one and a half meters in y tells us location relative to an origin. But a robot also needs to describe motion. How far did it travel? How long did the trip take? How quickly did it move? In which direction? And when will it reach a target? Today, we connect position to motion using distance, speed, time, and velocity. The central relationship is simple. For a constant-speed trip, distance equals average speed multiplied by elapsed time. If you know distance and time, average speed is distance divided by elapsed time. If you know distance and speed, elapsed time is distance divided by speed. Always include units. Distance might be measured in meters, centimeters, or millimeters. Time might be measured in seconds or milliseconds. Speed is distance per unit of time, such as meters per second or centimeters per second. Units are not decoration; they help check the equation. Meters per second multiplied by seconds leaves meters. The seconds cancel, and the result is a distance. Be careful with conversions. One meter per second is one hundred centimeters per second. One half of a second is five hundred milliseconds. Robotics programs often use seconds internally, even when sensors report milliseconds. Treating five hundred milliseconds as five hundred seconds, or treating half a second as half a millisecond, can create a major timing error. Now let’s distinguish speed from velocity. Speed tells us how fast something moves. It has size, but no direction, and average speed is never negative. Velocity includes direction. In one-dimensional motion, a plus or minus sign can show direction along an axis. In two or three dimensions, velocity is a vector with components and direction. Distance and displacement are also different. Distance is the total length of the route. Displacement is the change from the initial position to the final position, including direction. Here is a memorable example. RoboRover starts at one point, travels three meters east, then turns left and travels four meters north, taking ten seconds altogether. Its route distance is three plus four, or seven meters. But its displacement goes directly from the start to the finish. The displacement has components of three meters east and four meters north. Its magnitude is five meters, from the familiar three-four-five triangle. So the average speed is seven meters divided by ten seconds, or zero point seven meters per second. The magnitude of the average displacement velocity is five meters divided by ten seconds, or zero point five meters per second. The average velocity also has components: zero point three meters per second east and zero point four meters per second north. The route distance and the displacement answer different questions, so they cannot be used interchangeably. For a straight trip without reversing, average speed and the magnitude of average velocity have the same numerical value. Once a robot turns or reverses, they may differ. Here is a second worked example focused on timing. RoboRover begins at x equals one point two meters and drives forward to x equals four point eight meters in four seconds. The change in position is four point eight minus one point two, which is three point six meters. Since the path is straight and the robot does not reverse, its route distance is also three point six meters. Average speed is therefore three point six meters divided by four seconds, or zero point nine meters per second. Because the motion is in the positive x direction, the average velocity is positive zero point nine meters per second. If an ideal model maintains a constant velocity of positive zero point nine meters per second, traveling another one point eight meters would take one point eight divided by zero point nine, or two seconds. Pause and predict: If RoboRover travels six meters in three seconds, what is its average speed? And if it moves at zero point four meters per second for five seconds, how far does it travel? Take a brief beat. The answers are two meters per second and two meters. The first uses distance divided by time. The second uses speed multiplied by time. The Python lab turns this constant-velocity model into a simple simulation. It stores a starting position, a target position, a modeled velocity in meters per second, and a time step. It then calculates the ideal arrival time, updates position at regular intervals, records the values, and can plot position against time. The simulation uses the equation: current position equals starting position plus velocity multiplied by time. With a starting position of zero meters, a velocity of zero point seven five meters per second, and a target of four point five meters, the predicted arrival time is six seconds. If the time step is one half-second, the simulation reaches the target after twelve intervals, while recording thirteen positions because it includes the starting position at time zero. The graph should be a straight rising line. Equal time steps produce regularly spaced position samples when velocity is constant. The lab also uses assertions to check assumptions and verify the final time and position. If plotting support is unavailable, the simulation can still print and verify its numerical results. The most important engineering caveat is this: a commanded motor setting is not automatically a calibrated velocity. A value such as motor power sixty does not necessarily mean zero point six meters per second. To use a velocity in a physical prediction, it must be measured or established through calibration, and the relationship can change with battery voltage, floor friction, payload, wheel diameter, direction, and wheel slip. A timed command is not automatically exact either. A robot may accelerate, stop gradually, veer because its motors differ, or receive delayed sensor readings.
