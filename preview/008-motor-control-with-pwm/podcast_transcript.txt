Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 8: Motor Control with PWM. In Class 7, RoboRover used a motor to turn electrical energy into motion. Today, we move from simply running a motor to controlling how strongly it is driven. Our main tool is pulse-width modulation, or PWM. PWM rapidly switches a command between states. The important quantity is duty cycle: the fraction of each repeating period spent in the on, or commanded-drive, state. A 25 percent duty cycle means the command is on for one-quarter of every period and off for the remaining three-quarters. This class focuses on brushed DC motors controlled by an H-bridge or another low-side motor driver. Keep the signal chain in mind. A controller or computer sends a low-power logic command. The motor driver responds by switching the higher current needed by the motor. That creates voltage at the motor terminals, current through the windings, torque at the shaft, and eventually motion at the wheel. The controller usually does not power the motor directly. A computer pin is a logic output, not a motor power supply. The driver is the interface that handles the motor’s operating, startup, and possibly stall current. Here is the central idea. Imagine a very fast switch connected to a lamp. If the switch is on for half of every period, the duty cycle is 50 percent. If it is on for one-quarter, the duty cycle is 25 percent. The switch does not create a weaker on-state voltage. In our simplified model, the power stage switches between zero volts and the supply voltage. Duty cycle changes how long the full supply voltage is applied. For a 10 millisecond period, a 50 percent duty cycle means 5 milliseconds on and 5 milliseconds off. An 80 percent duty cycle means 8 milliseconds on and 2 milliseconds off. The pulse height stays the same; only the time fraction changes. The period is the time from the start of one pulse to the start of the next. Frequency is how many periods occur each second. They are related by frequency equals one divided by period. A frequency of 50 hertz means 50 complete patterns per second. For the simplified zero-to-supply waveform, the ideal time-average is duty cycle multiplied by supply voltage. So, with a 12 volt supply and a 40 percent duty cycle, the ideal average of that assumed waveform is 4.8 volts. Now for a memorable RoboRover example. Suppose the rover has a 9 volt battery, uses a 50 hertz PWM frequency, and receives a 40 percent duty-cycle command. First, find the period. One divided by 50 hertz is 0.020 seconds, or 20 milliseconds. Next, find the on-time. Forty percent of 20 milliseconds is 8 milliseconds. That leaves 12 milliseconds off. Finally, calculate the ideal waveform average: 40 percent of 9 volts is 3.6 volts. So the timing model is 8 milliseconds on and 12 milliseconds off, repeating every 20 milliseconds. The ideal average of the assumed power-stage waveform is 3.6 volts. But here is the most important engineering caveat: that 3.6 volts is not automatically the voltage measured across the motor terminals, and it certainly is not a guarantee of a particular speed. Real H-bridges may actively drive the motor, let current recirculate, apply braking, or allow the motor terminals to float during the off interval. The driver topology and PWM mode determine the details. There is another critical distinction. Winding current does not usually jump instantly, because the motor’s inductance resists sudden current changes. Torque depends strongly on winding current. Speed depends on torque balance, load, friction, back EMF, inertia, battery voltage, and losses. Increasing duty cycle often increases speed under a given condition, but duty cycle is not a direct revolutions-per-minute setting. A 50 percent command does not guarantee half of maximum speed. Pause and predict: if the duty cycle rises from 20 percent to 60 percent while the supply stays at 9 volts, does the ideal average increase or decrease? Take a moment. The answer is increase. The ideal averages are 1.8 volts at 20 percent and 5.4 volts at 60 percent. Remember, those are averages of the simplified waveform, not promises about the real motor. PWM commonly controls the magnitude of motor drive. Direction usually requires an H-bridge or another circuit that can reverse the voltage across the motor. In this class, assume direction has already been selected. The choice of PWM frequency also involves engineering trade-offs. The 50 hertz example is convenient for arithmetic and visualization, but it is audible and may create noticeable torque or current ripple. A frequency such as 20 kilohertz may move switching noise above much of the audible range, but it can increase switching losses, heating, electromagnetic interference, and demands on the driver. The correct value depends on the motor, driver topology, inductance, current ripple, and manufacturer limits. The Python lab makes these ideas visible without pretending to be a complete motor simulator. You enter a duty cycle, and the program creates an idealized repeating power-stage waveform. It also displays a simple first-order speed response. In that teaching model, target speed is assumed to be proportional to duty cycle, while the simulated speed approaches the target gradually. A time constant controls how quickly it approaches. Try conceptual comparisons at 20, 60, and 100 percent duty cycle. The ideal waveform average and the model’s final target change with duty cycle. The configured response time does not. At 100 percent, the idealized waveform stays high for the entire period. After one time constant, the first-order model reaches approximately 63 percent of the final change. That model omits resistance, inductance, back EMF, load, friction, current limiting, driver losses, topology, and heating. It is useful for understanding command timing and gradual response, not for predicting the exact behavior of a physical rover. In a real robot, load and friction matter immediately. A heavier box may require more torque. Static friction or gearbox friction may prevent movement at a low duty cycle.
