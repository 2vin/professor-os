Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 9: Gears: Trading Speed for Strength. In Class 8, RoboRover used PWM, or pulse-width modulation, to control the average electrical power sent to its motor. A higher duty cycle can usually make the motor turn faster or push harder, up to the motor’s limits. But a motor shaft is often not connected directly to a robot’s wheel. Engineers may place gears between them. Gears let a robot trade one mechanical quantity for another: less rotational speed can produce more turning strength, while more speed usually means less torque. A useful mental model is this: PWM chooses how strongly we ask the motor to work. Gears choose how that work is exchanged between speed and turning strength. A gear is a toothed wheel that transfers rotation to another toothed wheel. The gear connected to the motor is the driver gear. The gear receiving motion is the driven gear. When a small driver gear turns a larger driven gear, the larger gear turns more slowly and produces more torque under the ideal model. The two external gears also rotate in opposite directions. We calculate the gear ratio using the number of teeth on the driven gear divided by the number of teeth on the driver gear. So, if a 12-tooth driver meshes with a 36-tooth driven gear, the ratio is 36 divided by 12, which equals 3. This is called a 3:1 reduction. The driver must rotate three times for the driven gear to rotate once. Pause and predict: if the motor turns at 180 revolutions per minute and this is a 3:1 reduction, how fast does the driven gear turn? Take a brief beat. The answer is 60 revolutions per minute, because the input speed is divided by three. Under the ideal model, torque is multiplied by three. Let’s work through the same example more realistically. Suppose the motor is operating at 180 revolutions per minute with a motor-shaft torque of 0.08 newton-metres. The gear ratio is 3, and mechanical efficiency is 0.85, meaning 85 percent of the ideal mechanical output remains available after losses. The output speed is 180 divided by 3, or 60 revolutions per minute. Ideal output torque would be 3 times 0.08, or 0.24 newton-metres. Including efficiency, realistic output torque is 0.85 times 3 times 0.08, which equals 0.204 newton-metres. If the driven gear is rigidly attached to a wheel, that 0.204 newton-metres is also the simplified wheel-shaft torque in this model. Suppose the wheel radius is 0.03 metres. The available tangential force at the wheel rim is torque divided by radius: 0.204 divided by 0.03, which equals approximately 6.8 newtons. So this gearing makes the wheel turn at 60 rather than 180 revolutions per minute, while increasing available wheel torque from the motor-shaft value of 0.08 to approximately 0.204 newton-metres. The simplified wheel-rim force is about 6.8 newtons. The most important engineering caveat is that this is an operating-point estimate, not a complete motor or vehicle simulation. Real gears lose energy through friction, tooth deformation, bearing friction, and imperfect alignment. More importantly, a motor’s speed and torque are coupled to current, voltage, temperature, and load. If the motor slows, reaches a current limit, or overheats, the assumed torque may not be available continuously. A gear train does not create energy. When it increases torque, it reduces speed enough to keep power approximately balanced. Mechanical power is torque multiplied by angular speed. Efficiency means output power is less than input power. That is why a robot cannot gain both unlimited speed and unlimited strength from gearing. Sometimes engineers use multiple stages. If one stage has a 2:1 reduction and a second has a 3:1 reduction, the total ratio is 2 times 3, or 6:1. Each additional stage can add friction, noise, weight, and alignment problems. Two ordinary external gear meshes reverse rotation twice, so the final output rotates in the same direction as the motor. Now consider RoboRover climbing a short cardboard ramp while carrying a sensor box. With a 1:1 gear pair, the motor turns the wheel quickly, but the robot struggles. A 2:1 reduction cuts ideal speed in half and approximately doubles ideal torque. A 3:1 reduction cuts ideal speed to one-third and provides the greatest torque multiplication. Using the earlier motor and wheel assumptions, the 1:1 choice produces about 2.27 newtons of simplified wheel-rim force. The 2:1 choice produces about 4.53 newtons. The 3:1 choice produces 6.8 newtons. If the simplified climbing requirement is at least 5.0 newtons, only the 3:1 choice qualifies. It is therefore also the fastest qualifying choice in that particular comparison. But the strongest ratio is not automatically the best ratio. Engineers also consider desired top speed, motor heating, battery power, wheel traction, physical space, gear noise, and wear. A robot that climbs well may be too slow on flat ground. There is another serious concern: stall. If the wheel cannot turn, the motor may draw high current and heat quickly. A larger reduction ratio can make it easier for the wheel to resist motion, but it does not make the motor immune to overload. RoboRover should not simply keep increasing PWM when its wheel is stuck. Practical designs may use current limiting, temperature monitoring, a mechanical fuse, or software that detects an abnormal condition.
