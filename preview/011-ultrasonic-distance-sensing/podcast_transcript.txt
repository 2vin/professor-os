Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 11, Ultrasonic Distance Sensing. In the previous class, we learned that sensors convert parts of the physical world into measurable signals. Today, RoboRover learns to estimate distance using sound. An ultrasonic distance sensor sends out a short sound pulse and measures how long it takes for the echo to return. Ultrasonic means above the normal human hearing range. Many common modules send a burst near 40 kilohertz, although hardware varies, so real programs should follow the sensor’s documented specifications. The central idea is called time of flight, or TOF. Imagine shouting in a canyon. Your voice travels outward, hits a surface, and returns as an echo. If you know the speed of sound and the time for that round trip, you can estimate how far away the surface is. An ultrasonic sensor usually has two visible parts: a transmitter, which sends the pulse, and a receiver, which detects the returning echo. The sensor does not directly measure distance. It directly measures a time interval. The robot then applies a model: measured echo time, plus an assumed speed of sound, gives an estimated range. Here is the equation in speakable form. The distance, or range, equals the speed of sound multiplied by the echo time, divided by two. Why divide by two? Because the measured time includes two journeys: from the sensor to the object, and from the object back to the sensor. If the one-way distance is d, the sound travels a total distance of two d. So two d equals speed times time, and solving for d gives speed times time divided by two. At room temperature, a useful teaching approximation for the speed of sound in air is 343 metres per second. This value changes with temperature and, to a smaller degree in ordinary classroom conditions, with humidity and other air conditions. Pause and predict. If RoboRover receives an echo 0.010 seconds after sending the pulse, has the sound travelled one metre, two metres, or some other distance? Remember, that is the full outward-and-return journey. Brief beat. The total path is about 3.43 metres, using 343 metres per second. Dividing by two gives an estimated one-way range of about 1.715 metres. So it is not correct to report 3.43 metres as the distance to the object. Let’s work through a more precise example. Suppose the echo time is 0.0085 seconds. Multiply 343 metres per second by 0.0085 seconds. The result is 2.9155 metres, which is the round-trip distance. Divide by two, and the estimated range is 1.45775 metres, or approximately 1.46 metres. Notice how the units behave: metres per second multiplied by seconds leaves metres. Now consider RoboRover moving toward a cardboard box. Its sensor measures an echo time of 5.8 milliseconds. The speed equation requires seconds, so convert milliseconds first. 5.8 milliseconds is 0.0058 seconds. Now calculate 343 multiplied by 0.0058, then divide by two. The result is 0.9947 metres, or about 99.5 centimetres. The box is approximately one metre away. If RoboRover’s rule is to stop when the estimated range is below 0.60 metres, this particular reading should not trigger a stop. But that does not mean the robot can trust one measurement as perfect truth. It must also consider noise, braking distance, calibration error, and whether the object reflects sound reliably. This is an important distinction between range and simple detection. A proximity switch might report only “object present.” An ultrasonic sensor can report an estimated range, such as approximately 0.62 metres. Real sensors also have minimum and maximum useful ranges. Very close objects may return an echo before the electronics are ready, while distant echoes may be too weak to detect. In the Python lab, you will simulate RoboRover approaching a wall. The simulation starts with true distances, converts each distance into a round-trip echo time, and converts those times back into estimated ranges. Small offsets are added to represent measurement noise or calibration error. The program then compares each estimated range with a stop threshold and plots actual distance alongside estimated distance. You do not need to read the program line by line to understand the main structure. One part uses the equation time equals two times distance divided by speed. Another uses distance equals speed times time divided by two. A list of offsets makes the estimated readings differ slightly from the true readings. Automated checks confirm important numerical results, and a horizontal graph line marks the software’s stop threshold. That line represents a decision boundary, not a physical wall. You can also predict actions from echo times. With a stop threshold of 0.60 metres, an echo time of 2.9 milliseconds corresponds to roughly 0.498 metres, so the action should be STOP. An echo time of 4.4 milliseconds corresponds to a distance a little below 0.76 metres, so the action should be GO. A 6.0 millisecond echo corresponds to slightly more than one metre, also GO. The pattern is simple: longer echo time means a farther object, and shorter echo time means a nearer object. Now for the most important engineering caveat: an ultrasonic reading is an estimate shaped by the environment. Sound spreads into a cone rather than travelling as a perfectly narrow beam. A nearby object at an angle may reflect the pulse before a more distant wall does. A soft material such as thick fabric or foam may absorb sound. An angled, smooth surface may reflect the sound away from the receiver, producing no useful echo or causing the sensor to detect something farther away. Motor vibration can shake the sensor and change its angle. Multiple surfaces can create confusing echoes. Measurements taken too quickly can overlap with echoes from earlier pulses. Temperature also matters because it changes the speed of sound. For basic obstacle avoidance, that error may be acceptable. For precise measurement, calibration and temperature compensation become more important.
