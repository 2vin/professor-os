Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 13: Encoders: Measuring Wheel Motion. RoboRover has already learned to use infrared sensors to notice nearby objects and differences in reflected light. Those sensors can help answer questions like, “Is something close?” or “Does this surface appear dark or bright?” But sensing the outside world is only half of navigation. RoboRover also needs to estimate what its wheels have done. That is the job of an encoder. An encoder is a sensor that measures wheel rotation by producing countable electrical signals. These counted events are called ticks, or counts. From them, we can estimate how far a wheel turned, how far the robot traveled, how much it rotated, and where it probably is now. That word, probably, is important. Wheel measurements are useful, but they are not a perfect record of reality. The process of estimating position from wheel motion is called odometry. The central chain is simple: encoder ticks become wheel angle; wheel angle becomes distance along the wheel’s circumference; and the left and right wheel distances become an estimate of robot motion. Imagine a small flag attached to a bicycle wheel. Every time the flag passes a fixed mark, you count one event. More events mean more rotation. Real encoders use patterns such as marked disks or magnetic regions, but the reasoning is similar. Suppose one complete wheel revolution produces N ticks, and the encoder reports n ticks. The fraction of a revolution is n divided by N. Since one full revolution is two pi radians, the wheel angle is that fraction multiplied by two pi. To convert rotation into distance, use the wheel circumference. Circumference equals two pi times the wheel radius. Therefore, the distance for n ticks is n divided by N, multiplied by two pi, multiplied by the radius. The sign matters too. Under a chosen convention, positive ticks may mean forward and negative ticks may mean backward. The software must know that convention from the wiring and encoder setup. Here is a worked example. Suppose RoboRover has a wheel diameter of ten centimeters, so its radius is five centimeters, or zero point zero five meters. The encoder produces 360 ticks per revolution, and it reports 90 ticks. Ninety divided by 360 is one quarter. So the wheel turned one quarter of a revolution, which is 90 degrees, or pi over two radians. The wheel circumference is two pi times zero point zero five meters, which is zero point one pi meters. One quarter of that is zero point zero two five pi meters, approximately zero point zero seven eight five meters. So the wheel’s contact point traveled about 7.85 centimeters. Now consider both wheels on a differential-drive robot. If the left and right wheels travel equal distances forward, the robot is estimated to move straight. If the right wheel travels farther than the left, the robot curves toward the left. If the left travels farther, it curves toward the right. If one wheel moves forward while the other moves backward by the same amount, the robot rotates in place. Let’s use the same wheel radius, 0.05 meters, with 360 ticks per revolution. Let the track width—the distance between the effective contact points of the two wheels—be 0.16 meters. During one interval, both wheels report 180 ticks. Each wheel travels half a revolution, or approximately 0.1571 meters. Since the distances are equal, the estimated forward motion is their average: approximately 0.1571 meters, with no heading change. Now for a pause and predict. If the left wheel reports 90 ticks and the right wheel reports negative 90 ticks, does RoboRover move forward, rotate in place, or do both? …Brief beat… The answer is: it rotates in place. The left wheel moves forward about 0.0785 meters, while the right wheel moves backward by the same amount. Their average is zero, so the estimated translation is zero. Their difference produces a heading change. Dividing the right-minus-left distance by the track width gives approximately negative zero point nine eight radians, or about 56.25 degrees clockwise under this coordinate convention. That is the heart of differential-drive odometry: the average wheel distance estimates forward motion, while the difference between wheel distances estimates turning. The Python lab simulates this process conceptually. It starts RoboRover at an initial position and heading, converts each pair of left and right encoder readings into wheel distances, averages those distances for forward motion, and uses their difference divided by track width for the heading change. For each short interval, it uses a midpoint heading to update the estimated x and y position. The program then plots the estimated path and uses assertions to check that the numerical results agree with the model. The simulation has three steps: both wheels move one revolution and the robot travels straight; the wheels move equal amounts in opposite directions and the robot rotates in place; then both wheels move half a revolution and the robot travels forward in its new direction. The plot is an estimate, not a camera recording of the true path. There is an important engineering caveat. Odometry gradually becomes inaccurate because real systems are imperfect. Wheels can slip, especially during turns. Wheel diameters may not match exactly. Encoder resolution limits how finely small motions can be measured. Gear backlash, loose wheels, incorrect track-width calibration, missed electrical counts, and body flex can all introduce error. Even if the encoder counts perfectly, a wheel can spin on a slippery surface while the robot moves very little. The encoder tells us how much the wheel rotated; it does not directly tell us where the robot is relative to the room. These errors accumulate, so odometry may be excellent over a short interval but drift over a long journey. That is why practical robots combine wheel odometry with sensors such as infrared sensors, cameras, range sensors, or inertial sensors. A common mistake is to treat one tick as a fixed distance, such as one centimeter. It is not.
