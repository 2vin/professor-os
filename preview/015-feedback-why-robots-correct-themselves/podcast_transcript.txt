Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 15: Feedback: Why Robots Correct Themselves. In the previous class, RoboRover followed a virtual line. Today, we name the repeated process that makes correction possible: feedback. The central pattern is simple: measure, compare, correct, and measure again. Imagine RoboRover drifting to the right of a black line. It measures its position, steers left, measures again, and changes its response as the situation changes. That repeated measurement-and-correction cycle is a feedback loop. For this lesson, RoboRover uses a simplified abstract position sensor. It reports a signed estimate of lateral position relative to the desired line. This is a model variable. It does not mean that one ordinary light sensor directly measures signed position in centimeters. Physical line-following robots commonly infer position from two or more reflectance sensors, along with calibration and knowledge of the sensor arrangement. Our convention is straightforward. Right of the line is positive, so the robot should steer left. Left of the line is negative, so it should steer right. Centered is zero, so it should drive straight. A feedback system has four useful parts. First, the desired state: what should happen? Second, the measurement: what is happening now? Third, the error: how different are those two states? And fourth, the correction: what action should reduce the difference? Suppose the target position is zero centimeters, and RoboRover measures plus six centimeters. The error is target minus measured position: zero minus six, which equals negative six centimeters. The negative sign tells us that the robot is to the right of the target under this convention, so the correction should be leftward. Notice that error is not automatically a correction. Error describes the condition. The controller converts that information into an action. In today’s simplified controller, a measured position greater than plus two centimeters selects left. A measured position less than negative two centimeters selects right. Anything in between selects straight. That middle range is called a deadband. It prevents the robot from reacting to every tiny change, including small measurement noise. The tradeoff is that the robot may remain slightly offset instead of reaching exactly zero. Here is a worked example. Start with a target of zero centimeters and a measured position of plus six centimeters. The error is negative six, so RoboRover chooses left. After the correction, suppose the next measurement is plus two point four centimeters. The error is now negative two point four. The sign is still negative, so the robot is still to the right and continues left. But the magnitude has decreased from six to two point four, so the robot is closer. Suppose the next measurement is plus one point five centimeters. That lies inside the two-centimeter deadband, so the action becomes straight. If the robot later crosses to negative two point five centimeters, the error becomes positive two point five, and the correction changes to right. The sign matters because it identifies the direction of the needed correction. Pause and predict: with a threshold of two centimeters, what actions correspond to measured positions of plus two point one, plus two point zero, and plus one point nine centimeters? Brief beat. The answer is left, straight, and straight. The comparisons are strict. A value exactly at plus two is inside the deadband. This is feedback because a measurement influences a current or future action. Open-loop control does not use the result to adjust what happens next. For example, “Drive both motors for three seconds, then stop” is open-loop. “Measure position, steer to reduce error, measure again, and choose again” is feedback. Feedback is not the same as autonomy. A teleoperated robot can use feedback in its motor controllers. An industrial robot can follow a preprogrammed task while its joints use feedback. These describe different aspects of a system. The Python lab models this loop deterministically. It starts RoboRover at six centimeters, uses a target of zero, applies a fixed movement of zero point eight centimeters for each left or right command, and uses a predetermined list of sensor-noise values. The program records actual position, measured position, error, and action at every step. Conceptually, each iteration measures the current actual position with added noise, calculates the signed error, chooses left, right, or straight, records the result, and updates the actual position. The state update is actual next equals actual current plus step size times command. The command is negative one for left, positive one for right, and zero for straight. With the supplied data, the original run produces five left actions followed by five straight actions. The final actual position is two centimeters. The measurements are not always equal to the actual position because noise changes the sensor report. The controller makes decisions from the measured position, while the verification checks the actual position. You can modify the simulation to see the consequences. Removing correction leaves the final position at six centimeters. Making the deadband wider, from two to five centimeters, causes the robot to stop correcting earlier, at five point two centimeters. Adding a large measurement disturbance causes an extra left correction and produces a final position of one point two centimeters. That disturbance changes the timing of the correction, not its fixed strength. This distinction is important. Measurement noise changes what the robot believes it sees. A process disturbance, such as wheel slip, changes the physical state itself. The most important engineering caveat is that this simulation is not reality. Its fixed zero point eight centimeter movement ignores battery voltage, friction, wheel mismatch, heading, actuator limits, delay, and overshoot. A real robot may coast, slip, turn gradually, or respond late. Delayed feedback can cause overshoot or oscillation, and poorly chosen corrections can produce repeated left-right switching. Test the direction mapping at very low speed, and use appropriate hardware safety: keep hands, hair, clothing, and cables away from wheels, and make sure the stop command or power disconnect is ready before motion begins.
