Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 17, PI and PID Intuition. In the previous class, RoboRover used proportional, or P, control. It measured the current error, multiplied that error by a gain, and produced a motor command. If the rover was zero point four metres short of its target, P control produced a command proportional to zero point four metres. P control is simple and useful, but it has an important limitation. Suppose the rover is climbing a gentle ramp. At the target, the position error is zero, so proportional control requests zero command. Yet the ramp is still pulling the rover backward. The rover may therefore settle slightly below the target, because it needs some remaining error to create the command that opposes the disturbance. Today we add two more terms. Integral, or I, control accumulates error over time. Derivative, or D, control responds to the measured rate at which error changes. Together with P, these form a PID controller. The three terms ask different questions. P asks, “How wrong are we now?” I asks, “How much error has accumulated?” And D asks, “How quickly is the error changing?” Imagine RoboRover approaching a painted stopping line. P responds to the rover’s current distance from the line. I keeps a running tally of error that has persisted. D responds to whether the remaining error is shrinking quickly or slowly. When the rover is approaching rapidly, the derivative contribution can act like braking. Pause and predict. The rover is zero point twenty metres short of its target and is moving toward it quickly. Using error equal to target minus measured position, what signs should the three terms have? P should be positive, because the rover is short of the target. I should be positive if that shortfall has persisted. D should be negative, because the error is shrinking. A brief beat. That is the basic intuition: P pushes because of present error, I builds correction for persistent error, and D subtracts command when the approach is becoming too rapid. But larger gains are not automatically better. Excessive gains can cause overshoot, oscillation, actuator saturation, or strong responses to measurement noise. Let’s work through one complete calculation. The target is two point zero metres, and the current measured position is one point six metres. The current error is therefore zero point four metres. Suppose the previous error was zero point five metres, the previous integral value was zero point twelve metre-seconds, and the sample interval is zero point ten seconds. The updated integral is the old integral plus current error times the sample interval. That is zero point twelve plus zero point four times zero point ten, giving zero point sixteen metre-seconds. The derivative estimate is the change in error divided by the sample interval. The error changed from zero point five to zero point four metres, so the derivative is zero point four minus zero point five, divided by zero point ten, or negative one point zero metres per second. Now choose gains of twenty percent per metre for P, four percent per metre-second for I, and two percent per metre-per-second for D. The P contribution is twenty times zero point four, or eight percent. The I contribution is four times zero point sixteen, or zero point sixty-four percent. The D contribution is two times negative one point zero, or negative two percent. Add them together: eight plus zero point sixty-four minus two equals six point sixty-four percent command. P and I push the rover forward because it is short of the target. D subtracts command because the remaining error is shrinking. The integral term is the controller’s memory. In continuous mathematics, it is the accumulated error over time. In a digital controller, it is updated as the previous integral plus error times the sample interval. For example, an error of zero point one metres held for five seconds produces approximately zero point five metre-seconds of accumulated error. That accumulated value can eventually provide the command needed to oppose constant friction, a slope, or a load. The derivative term estimates trend. If error decreases from zero point fifty metres to zero point forty metres in zero point ten seconds, the derivative is negative one point zero metres per second. The negative sign means the error is getting smaller. This lesson differentiates error, but some controllers differentiate measured position instead. The two forms are closely related with opposite signs when the target is fixed. Differentiating error can produce derivative kick when the target changes suddenly. Differentiating the measurement avoids that particular reference-step kick, but it still needs careful filtering and tuning. The conceptual PID equation is P contribution plus I contribution plus D contribution. A PI controller simply omits the D term. People often say that PI eliminates steady-state error, but that statement has conditions. The actuator must have enough authority to oppose the disturbance, the disturbance must be within the practical operating range, and the closed loop must remain stable. Integral action cannot rescue a physically incapable actuator or an unstable design. The Python lab turns the concept into a sampled simulation. The loop runs every zero point zero two seconds. It updates the integral, estimates the derivative from consecutive measured errors, limits the requested command to minus one through plus one, and uses conditional integration as one simple anti-windup strategy. The simulated plant then updates velocity and position. It includes a constant backward disturbance, velocity drag, and optional deterministic measurement noise. The lab compares P, PI, and PID responses, individual P, I, and D contributions, raw commands before limiting, and commands actually applied after limiting. It also compares noisy PI and PID behavior. For the supplied gains, noisy PID should show more command variation because derivative action reacts to rapid measurement changes. The most important engineering caveat is that integral can wind up during saturation. If the motor is already at its maximum command, the integral may continue accumulating.
