Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 18: Robot Kinematics: Motion Without Forces. In the previous class, RoboRover learned about PI and PID control: comparing a desired value with a measured value, then adjusting motor commands to reduce the error. Today, we step back and ask a different question: if we know a robot’s motion command, where will the robot be, and how fast will it move? That is the central purpose of kinematics. Kinematics describes motion using position, orientation, velocity, and time. It does not directly explain the forces, motor torques, or friction that caused the motion. A common planar robot pose has three parts: x position, y position, and orientation, usually written as theta. The x and y positions describe where the robot is on a map, measured in metres. Theta describes which direction the robot faces, measured in radians. So a pose answers, “Where is the robot, and which way is it pointing?” Velocity answers a different question: “How is that pose changing right now?” Velocity includes direction, not just speed. A robot can move east, north, or in some other direction while having the same speed. It can also rotate. Angular velocity, represented by omega, tells us how quickly the robot’s orientation changes. Its units are radians per second. In this class, positive angular velocity means counterclockwise rotation. Now imagine graph paper on the floor. The world frame is fixed to the room. Its x-axis and y-axis do not rotate when RoboRover turns. The body frame is attached to the robot. Its x-axis points forward, and its y-axis points to the robot’s left. This distinction is essential. When RoboRover says, “Move forward at half a metre per second,” that command is naturally expressed in the body frame. On the room’s map, the same motion could point east, northeast, or north, depending on the robot’s orientation. If the robot moves forward with speed v and does not slide sideways, its body-frame velocity has a forward component of v and a sideways component of zero. To express that motion in the world frame, we use the robot’s orientation. The world-frame x velocity is v times the cosine of theta, and the world-frame y velocity is v times the sine of theta. For the more general planar case, the rate of change of x combines the body-frame forward and sideways velocities after rotation by theta. The rate of change of y does the same. The rate of change of theta is omega. For a wheeled robot that only moves forward and rotates, the equations simplify to: x changes at v times cosine theta, y changes at v times sine theta, and theta changes at omega. If omega is zero, the robot travels in a straight line. If forward speed and angular velocity are both constant and nonzero, the robot follows a circular arc. A computer usually calculates this motion in small time steps. For each step, it adds the current x rate multiplied by the time step to x, does the same for y, and adds angular velocity multiplied by the time step to theta. This is a numerical approximation. Smaller time steps usually represent changing motion more accurately, but require more computation. Here is a worked example. RoboRover starts at x equals 1 metre, y equals 2 metres, and theta equals zero radians. It drives forward at 0.50 metres per second while rotating counterclockwise at 0.20 radians per second for 3 seconds. First, its orientation change is angular velocity times time: 0.20 times 3.0, which equals 0.60 radians. Its travelled distance along the curved path is speed times time: 0.50 times 3.0, or 1.50 metres. The turning radius is speed divided by angular velocity: 0.50 divided by 0.20, which equals 2.50 metres. Using the exact circular-arc equations, the change in x is approximately 1.412 metres, and the change in y is approximately 0.702 metres. So the final pose is approximately x equals 2.412 metres, y equals 2.702 metres, and theta equals 0.600 radians. Notice what happened. RoboRover moved mostly east because it began facing east. It also moved north because it gradually turned counterclockwise. Its path length was 1.50 metres, but its straight-line displacement was shorter because the path curved. Pause and predict: if the angular velocity became negative while the forward speed stayed positive, which way would the path bend? Take a brief moment. The answer is: it would bend in the opposite direction. Negative angular velocity means clockwise rotation instead of counterclockwise rotation. The Python lab simulates this same worked example. Conceptually, it stores the robot’s pose, applies an exact circular-arc update at each time step, records the positions, checks the final values, and plots the path. The lab also handles the special case where angular velocity is effectively zero, because dividing by a nearly zero value would be unsafe. You can experiment by setting angular velocity to zero, reversing its sign, increasing speed, or increasing turn rate, and predict how the path changes before viewing the plot. The most important engineering caveat is that a motion command is not necessarily the robot’s actual motion. A command for 0.50 metres per second does not guarantee that speed on the ground. Wheels can slip, motors can be unequal, wheel diameter or wheel spacing can be miscalibrated, commands can arrive late, and mechanical limits can prevent the requested motion. Repeated numerical approximations can also create position error. This is why kinematics is useful but not sufficient by itself. It provides a fast, understandable ideal model. A PI or PID speed controller can help a robot achieve a desired wheel or motor speed, but it cannot automatically guarantee that the robot’s estimated pose is correct. Motion control and pose estimation are related, but separate engineering tasks. As a practical takeaway, whenever you study a robot motion problem, ask two questions. First, which coordinate frame is being used: the fixed world frame or the moving body frame? Second, is the quantity a state, a velocity, a command, or a measurement?
