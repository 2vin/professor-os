Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 19, Differential Drive Robots. In the previous class, we learned kinematics: how to describe motion using position, direction, speed, and turning rate, without yet discussing the forces that produce that motion. Today, we connect those ideas to one of the most common mobile robot designs: the differential drive robot. RoboRover has two powered wheels, one on the left and one on the right. By changing the speeds of those wheels, RoboRover can move straight, follow a curve, or spin in place. The central idea is simple: the robot’s motion depends on the relationship between the left-wheel speed and the right-wheel speed. We’ll use a consistent sign convention. Forward wheel speed is positive, and reverse speed is negative. Positive angular velocity means counterclockwise rotation, which we’ll describe as turning left. The two wheels are separated by a distance called the wheel track, or wheel separation, represented by b. If both wheels have the same speed, RoboRover moves in a straight line. That line may be forward or backward, depending on whether the shared speed is positive or negative. If the right wheel is faster than the left wheel, the right side travels farther around the outside of a curve, and the robot turns left. If the left wheel is faster, the robot turns right. Finally, if the wheels move at equal speeds in opposite directions, one forward and one backward, the center of the robot stays in place while the body spins. You can picture this by imagining a book on a table. Push both sides forward equally, and the book moves straight. Push the right side farther than the left, and the book rotates toward the left. Now let’s connect wheel speeds to the robot’s kinematic variables. The robot’s signed longitudinal velocity, called v, is the average of the two signed wheel speeds. In plain language, add the right-wheel speed and the left-wheel speed, then divide by two. The turning rate, called omega, is the right-wheel speed minus the left-wheel speed, divided by the wheel separation. The units work out correctly. A speed difference has units of meters per second. Dividing by a distance in meters gives inverse seconds, reported here as radians per second. These values then connect to the pose equations from the previous class. The rate of change of x is v times the cosine of the heading. The rate of change of y is v times the sine of the heading. And the rate of change of heading is omega. Pause and predict. Suppose the left wheel moves at zero point one meters per second, the right wheel at zero point three meters per second, and the wheel separation is zero point four meters. Does RoboRover move straight, curve, or spin? Which way does it turn? Take a moment. The answer is that it curves left. Its forward velocity is the average of zero point one and zero point three, which is zero point two meters per second. Its turning rate is the difference, zero point two, divided by zero point four, giving zero point five radians per second. Since that value is positive, the turn is counterclockwise, or leftward. Here is a worked example. RoboRover has a wheel separation of zero point three meters. Its left wheel travels at zero point two meters per second, and its right wheel travels at zero point five meters per second. First, calculate the forward velocity. Add zero point five and zero point two, then divide by two. The result is zero point three five meters per second. Next, calculate the turning rate. Subtract zero point two from zero point five, giving zero point three meters per second. Divide by the wheel separation of zero point three meters. The result is one point zero radian per second. Because it is positive, RoboRover turns left. To find the turning radius, divide forward velocity by turning rate. Zero point three five divided by one point zero gives a radius of zero point three five meters. So RoboRover’s center follows a circular arc of radius zero point three five meters, moving forward while turning left. After two seconds, the left wheel has traveled zero point four meters, the right wheel has traveled one point zero meters, and the robot’s heading has changed by two radians. There are two especially important special cases. When the wheel speeds are equal, their difference is zero, so the turning rate is zero and the path is straight. When the wheel speeds are equal and opposite, their average is zero, so the center has no forward or backward motion. But the difference is nonzero, so the robot rotates in place. The most important engineering caveat is that commanded wheel speeds are not always actual wheel speeds. The ideal equations assume no significant slipping. Real wheels may slip during fast turns or on smooth floors. Wheel diameters may differ slightly. Motors may produce unequal speeds, and mechanical play can affect motion. Battery voltage, payload, floor friction, and wheel wear also matter. There is another common source of confusion. These equations require wheel linear speed in meters per second, not motor speed in revolutions per minute.
