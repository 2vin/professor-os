Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 20, Odometry. In the previous class, RoboRover learned how a differential-drive robot moves. With independently driven left and right wheels, equal wheel travel produces straight motion. Unequal travel produces a turn, and opposite wheel motion can turn the robot in place. Today we use those wheel movements to answer a new question: where does RoboRover think it is now? That is the purpose of odometry. Odometry connects measured wheel motion to an estimate of the robot’s position and orientation. It is useful, inexpensive, and available continuously. But it is not perfect. Small errors accumulate, causing drift. The central idea is simple: odometry estimates motion by accumulating measurements from the robot’s own movement. This is also called dead reckoning. Imagine walking through a dark room while counting your steps and turns. You begin at a known doorway, take ten steps, turn left, walk six more steps, and turn right. Without seeing a landmark, you can estimate where you are. A robot does something similar using wheel encoders, which measure wheel rotation or encoder counts. The robot converts those measurements into inferred wheel travel, combines the left and right distances, and updates its estimated pose. A pose describes location and heading. In this class, we use x for eastward position, y for northward position, and theta for heading measured counterclockwise from the positive x-axis. So the pose is written as x, y, theta. It is important to distinguish a robot’s actual pose from odometry’s estimate of that pose. “Move forward” is a motor instruction. A pose such as x equals two meters, y equals one meter, and heading equals ninety degrees is an estimate of where the robot is and which way it faces. For a differential-drive robot, let delta s left be the distance traveled by the left wheel, delta s right be the distance traveled by the right wheel, and b be the track width, or distance between the wheel contact points. The approximate forward travel is the average of the two wheel distances. In plain language, add the left and right travel and divide by two. The heading change is the right-wheel distance minus the left-wheel distance, divided by the track width. Under our coordinate convention, a positive result means a counterclockwise, or leftward, turn. The units are meters divided by meters, which gives radians. To update position, we can use the heading halfway through the interval. Take the old heading and add half of the heading change. Then multiply the forward travel by the cosine of that midpoint heading to estimate the change in x. Multiply it by the sine of the midpoint heading to estimate the change in y. Finally, add the full heading change to obtain the new heading. This midpoint update is a practical approximation, especially when intervals are short and heading changes are small. For larger movements or turns, treating the entire interval as if it occurred at one midpoint heading becomes less accurate. Higher-accuracy systems can use exact differential-drive integration, which follows the circular arc implied by the two wheel distances. Here is a worked example. RoboRover starts at the origin, with a heading of thirty degrees counterclockwise from east. During one interval, the left wheel travels forty-eight hundredths of a meter, and the right wheel travels fifty-two hundredths of a meter. The track width is twenty hundredths of a meter. The average forward travel is fifty hundredths of a meter, or half a meter. The wheel difference is four hundredths of a meter. Divide that by the twenty-hundredths-meter track width, and the heading change is zero point two radians. Thirty degrees must be converted to radians before using Python’s trigonometric functions. Thirty degrees is approximately zero point five two four radians. The midpoint heading is therefore zero point five two four plus half of zero point two, or approximately zero point six two four radians. Using that midpoint heading, the estimated change in x is about zero point four zero five meters, and the estimated change in y is about zero point two nine three meters. The new heading is approximately zero point seven two four radians, or forty-one point five degrees. What does that mean physically? The right wheel traveled farther, so RoboRover turned left while moving forward. It moved about half a meter, but not in a perfectly straight line. And these numbers describe what the odometry calculation believes happened, not necessarily the exact physical motion. Pause and predict: if the right-wheel measurement were slightly too large, would odometry estimate too much leftward turning, too much rightward turning, or no change? Brief beat. The answer is too much leftward turning. The error may be small in one interval, but repeated errors gradually separate the estimated path from the physical path. There are two broad kinds of odometry error. Systematic error is a repeatable bias, such as one wheel being slightly larger or an encoder scale being miscalibrated. Random or changing error varies with conditions, such as floor texture, wheel slip, electrical noise, or changing contact with the ground. Both can make the estimated pose wrong, but they behave differently. The most important engineering caveat is that wheel encoders measure wheel rotation, not guaranteed ground motion. During a turn, a wheel may slip. The encoder can report plausible rotation even though the wheel’s contact point did not move as assumed. An incorrect track width, unequal wheel diameters, encoder conversion errors, uneven flooring, and motor differences can also produce drift. Coordinate conventions matter as well: swapping left and right, or reversing the positive rotation direction, can make the robot appear to turn the wrong way. The Python lab makes this visible conceptually. It compares a modeled true path—a mathematical reference square—with an odometry estimate. The estimate includes a two-percent distance-scale error and a one-degree error at every turn. The program applies wheel-motion updates, stores the path history, and plots both routes.
