Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 23, Moving Average Filters. In the previous class, we saw that RoboRover’s distance sensor can produce noisy measurements. The rover might actually be 100 centimeters from a wall, while the sensor reports 98, 103, 99, 101, and 97 centimeters. The wall is not rapidly moving. The measurements are imperfect. Today, we’ll use a simple tool for smoothing those readings: the moving average filter. A moving average combines several nearby measurements and calculates their average. Instead of trusting only the newest sensor value, RoboRover considers a short history. This often makes a noisy signal easier to interpret. But there is an important trade-off: a smoother signal usually responds more slowly to real changes. Imagine a small tray holding the latest three readings. RoboRover adds those readings, divides by three, and uses the result as its filtered distance. Then the tray moves forward by one sample. The oldest reading leaves, the newest reading enters, and the average is calculated again. That is why it is called a moving average. For a window of N samples, the filtered output is the newest reading plus the previous readings in the window, all divided by N. The window size is measured in samples. If the readings are in centimeters, the filtered output is also in centimeters. A window size of one performs no smoothing. A window size of three averages the newest three readings. A window size of ten averages the newest ten. Larger windows usually produce smoother signals, but they also keep older information in the calculation for longer. Let’s work through a concrete example. Suppose RoboRover records three distance readings: 48 centimeters, 52 centimeters, and 49 centimeters. Their average is 48 plus 52 plus 49, divided by three. That is 149 divided by three, or approximately 49.67 centimeters. Now the next reading is 55 centimeters. The window slides forward. The 48 leaves, and the window now contains 52, 49, and 55. Their average is 156 divided by three, which equals 52 centimeters. Notice what happened. The newest raw reading is 55 centimeters, but the filtered result is 52 centimeters. The filter did not immediately accept the entire change because two older readings were still included. Pause and predict: if the window size changes from three to one, will the filtered signal become smoother, or will it match the raw measurements? Take a moment. The answer is that it will match the raw measurements exactly. With a one-sample window, every output uses only the newest reading, so there is no smoothing. Now let’s examine a suspicious spike. Imagine these distance readings from RoboRover: 100, 104, 96, 101, 130, 99, and 102 centimeters. The 130-centimeter value is far from its neighbors. Perhaps the sensor briefly saw a reflective surface. Using a three-sample moving average, the output at reading three is the average of 100, 104, and 96, which equals 100 centimeters. At reading four, the average of 104, 96, and 101 is approximately 100.33 centimeters. At reading five, the average of 96, 101, and 130 is 109 centimeters. The spike has been reduced. The filtered result is 109, not 130. But the story does not end there. At reading six, the window contains 101, 130, and 99, producing 110 centimeters. At reading seven, it contains 130, 99, and 102, producing approximately 110.33 centimeters. This is the most important engineering caveat: a moving average does not identify and remove an unusual measurement. Instead, it spreads that measurement’s influence across several later outputs. The filter may prevent one sharp steering reaction, but it may also cause RoboRover to steer incorrectly for several samples. The same issue appears when the environment changes quickly. If a sudden obstacle appears closer to the rover, the raw distance may change almost immediately. The moving-average output falls more gradually because it still includes older, larger distances. Larger windows make the signal calmer, but they can also make the rover late. Sampling time helps us understand that delay. Suppose the sensor samples at 10 readings per second. The time between samples is 0.1 seconds. For a full five-sample window and a slowly changing signal, the approximate steady-state group delay is four divided by two, multiplied by 0.1 seconds, which equals 0.2 seconds. That is an approximation, not a universal rule. Startup behavior and abrupt changes, such as a sudden step, do not appear after one exact 0.2-second delay. A step change can be spread across several filtered outputs. At startup, the program may not yet have enough readings to fill the intended window. There are two common choices: wait until the window is full, or average whatever readings are currently available. In this lesson’s beginner implementation, the program uses the available readings. That gives an output immediately, but startup behaves slightly differently from steady operation. The Python lab demonstrates this conceptually with simulated distance data. A moving-average function selects the recent values, computes their average, and stores each filtered result. The lab also compares raw and filtered data on a plot, checks important results with assertions, and lets you try window sizes of one, three, and five. You should see that a larger window generally smooths the signal more, while a smaller window responds more quickly. The practical takeaway is to choose the window for the robot’s task, not just for visual smoothness. A larger window may be appropriate for a slowly changing temperature reading or a steadier display. It may be a poor choice for fast collision avoidance, where delay matters. Before using a filtered signal for a threshold or safety decision, validate the complete timing chain. Consider the sampling rate, the age of the measurements in the window, the controller’s timing, and the rover’s stopping distance. Also consider sensor failure modes such as reflections, dropouts, saturation, or impossible values. A moving average is a signal-processing tool. It is not automatically a controller, a decision-maker, or a complete solution to sensor problems. To recap: a moving average combines recent samples to reduce short-term variation.
