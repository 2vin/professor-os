Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 27, Contours and Shape Detection. In the previous class, RoboRover learned thresholding and segmentation. A camera image was converted into a simpler mask, where pixels were classified as likely foreground or background. That mask answers, “Which pixels belong to an object?” Today we ask the next question: “What shape does that group of pixels make?” We will use contours: boundaries around connected foreground regions. With contours, RoboRover can estimate area, perimeter, position, and rough shape. Those measurements can help distinguish a circular charging marker from a rectangular warning card or a triangular floor sign. The most important limitation is that these measurements are initially in pixel units. They describe how an object appears in the image, not its true physical size or three-dimensional position. We will return to that in the next class. Let’s begin with a quick prediction. If two bright objects touch in a binary mask, will a basic contour finder usually see one object or two? Brief pause. The usual answer is one connected contour, because there is no longer a background gap separating them. A contour is an ordered path around the edge of a connected foreground region. Think of a wet sponge pressed onto paper. The outline tells you where the sponge ends, even though the outline does not describe every detail inside it. For RoboRover, the pipeline is straightforward: camera image, thresholded mask, connected foreground regions, contours around those regions, and finally measurements and shape decisions. Imagine a white triangle on a black image. The filled white triangle is the region. The chain of pixels along its outside edge is the contour. A rectangle surrounding it is the bounding rectangle. A dot near the average location of its pixels is the centroid. And a simplified three-corner outline is a polygon approximation. OpenCV stores a contour as an ordered sequence of image points. Usually, x increases to the right, while y increases downward. That differs from the graph convention where y increases upward. Let’s review the main measurements. Contour area estimates how much image region lies inside the boundary. Its unit is pixels squared. Area is useful for rejecting tiny noise. For example, RoboRover might ignore contours smaller than 30 pixels squared. Perimeter, also called arc length, is the distance around the contour, measured in pixels. A bounding rectangle is the smallest axis-aligned rectangle containing the contour. It has a left coordinate, a top coordinate, a width, and a height. Axis-aligned means the rectangle remains horizontal and vertical rather than rotating to match the object. Its aspect ratio is width divided by height. Polygon approximation replaces a contour containing many small points with fewer straight-line segments. A triangle may reduce to three main corners, and a rectangle to four. The tolerance, often called epsilon, is measured in pixels. A larger tolerance produces a simpler polygon, but it may erase meaningful corners. Convexity is another clue. A contour is convex if it has no inward dents. A disk and an ordinary rectangle are convex, while a crescent is not. Image moments summarize how contour points or pixels are distributed. They can provide a centroid, or image-coordinate center. The centroid uses the first-order moments divided by the zeroth moment: the x coordinate is M ten divided by M zero zero, and the y coordinate is M zero one divided by M zero zero. The calculation must guard against M zero zero being zero, which can happen for a degenerate contour. The centroid is only an image-coordinate estimate. It is not automatically the physical center of the object in the real world. Now for a worked example. Suppose a rectangular floor marker appears 40 pixels wide and 25 pixels tall. Its idealized area is width times height: 40 times 25, or 1,000 pixels squared. Its perimeter is twice the sum of width and height: two times 65, or 130 pixels. Its aspect ratio is 40 divided by 25, which is 1.6. So the marker appears 1.6 times wider than it is tall. These values describe the image. If the camera moves closer, the same physical marker might appear 80 pixels wide and 50 pixels tall. Its image area would become approximately four times larger, even though the marker itself did not change. One additional shape measure is circularity. Circularity is four times pi times area, divided by perimeter squared. A perfect mathematical circle has a value of one. Real image measurements are often lower because of pixel stair-steps, lighting, and imperfect boundaries. Circularity is evidence, not a magical identity test. Suppose RoboRover detects three regions. Region A has an area of 18 pixels squared, Region B has an area of 960, and Region C has an area of 420. If the robot ignores anything smaller than 50 pixels squared, it rejects Region A as likely noise. Region B has a bounding box 40 pixels wide and 24 pixels high, giving an aspect ratio of about 1.67. It could be a rectangular marker. Region C has a bounding box 21 by 21, so its aspect ratio is one. Its circularity, using an area of 420 and a perimeter of 92, is about 0.62. That is not close to one. Region C might be a rounded square, a rough circle, or a distorted region. The engineering decision is not, “Circularity says circle.” It is, “This region is a possible circular marker, but another test or another camera view may be needed.” In OpenCV, contour retrieval mode also matters. External retrieval returns only outermost contours and omits hole boundaries. List retrieval returns all contours without parent-child hierarchy. Other modes preserve relationships between outer boundaries and holes, including deeper nested structures. For a ring, external retrieval gives only the outside boundary, while a hierarchy-preserving mode can expose the inner boundary as well.
