Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 32: Occupancy Grids. In the previous class, Mapping, RoboRover learned that a map is a representation of an environment. Today, we make that idea more precise with a common robotics map: the occupancy grid. An occupancy grid divides the world into many small square cells. Each cell records what the robot currently believes about a small patch of space. A cell can be free, meaning sensor evidence suggests the robot can pass through it along the observed beam, subject to the robot’s size and uncertainty. It can be occupied, meaning sensor evidence suggests an obstacle is present. Or it can be unknown, meaning the robot does not yet have enough information. The central idea is simple: one sensor measurement can provide information about several cells, not just the cell containing the robot. Imagine RoboRover driving through a room with a distance sensor pointing forward. The sensor reports that the nearest obstacle is one point five metres away. The space between RoboRover and that obstacle is probably free along the observed beam. The cell where the beam ends is probably occupied. Areas outside the beam remain unknown. Unknown is especially important. Unknown does not mean occupied, and it does not mean free. It means the robot has not gathered enough evidence. Think of an occupancy grid as graph paper placed over a room. The real world is continuous, but the grid approximates it with separate pieces. Smaller cells provide more detail, but require more memory and computation. Larger cells are cheaper, but may hide narrow obstacles or doorways. So an occupancy grid is not a perfect photograph. It is a structured record of evidence. Now let’s connect physical positions to grid cells. Suppose columns increase from left to right and rows increase from bottom to top. Let each square have width and height s metres. If the grid origin is at a known physical location, then a world position with coordinates x and y maps to a column equal to the floor of x divided by s, and a row equal to the floor of y divided by s. “Floor” means round down to the nearest integer. This convention matters. A cell in column i covers positions from i times s, inclusive, up to the next boundary, but not including it. Also, negative coordinates require care. The floor of negative zero point four is negative one, not zero. A real implementation must consistently define its origin, axis directions, boundary convention, and treatment of positions outside the map. Here is a worked example. Use cells that are zero point two metres wide. RoboRover is at x equal to zero point six metres and y equal to zero point four metres. Its sensor points east, toward increasing x, and detects an obstacle at x equal to one point four metres. The robot’s column is the floor of zero point six divided by zero point two, which is three. The obstacle’s column is the floor of one point four divided by zero point two, which is seven. Assuming both are in the same row, column three contains RoboRover and is treated as free. Columns four, five, and six are free along the beam. Column seven is occupied. The beam has provided evidence about five cells, including the robot’s cell. Notice what the robot does not know. It does not know that every other cell in the room is free. This one measurement supports only the cells along this particular direction. Pause and predict: if a beam travels through four cells and then detects an obstacle in the fifth, which cells should be marked free, and which should be marked occupied? Brief beat. The four cells before the detected obstacle are marked free along the observed beam. The fifth cell is marked occupied. The selected occupied cell is an approximation. The sensor return may come from the obstacle’s near surface or boundary, and not necessarily from the centre of the grid cell. A basic occupancy update follows the same pattern. Starting from the robot, the algorithm advances through cells along a sensor direction. It marks cells that the beam passes through as free. When it reaches the first detected obstacle, it marks that endpoint cell occupied and stops that ray. If the sensor reaches its maximum range without detecting an obstacle, inspected cells may be marked free, but there is no occupied endpoint. The Python lab demonstrates this information flow with a small simulated room. It creates a hidden environment containing boundary walls and a rectangular crate, then starts RoboRover with an entirely unknown occupancy map. Eight simple rays inspect nearby cells. The robot’s own cell is marked free, passing cells are marked free, and the first occupied cell on each ray is marked occupied. Unvisited cells remain unknown. The simulation uses integer labels: negative one for unknown, zero for free, and one for occupied. These are simple states, not probabilities. A real system may store confidence values and combine repeated observations. Also, the diagonal steps in the simulation are only a cell-neighbor approximation. A true diagonal beam can cross additional cells or pass near cell corners, so exact ray traversal requires a more careful geometric convention. The most important engineering caveat is that the map is only as reliable as the robot’s pose and sensing assumptions. If RoboRover’s estimated position or orientation is wrong, accurate sensor measurements can be inserted into the wrong cells. Sensor noise, reflective or transparent surfaces, moving objects, and limited viewing angles create further uncertainty. The robot’s physical footprint matters too. A cell may be free at its centre while the robot’s body would still collide with a nearby wall. Real navigation systems often inflate obstacle cells to keep the entire robot clear, not just its centre point. Resolution also affects storage. Imagine a physical region six metres by four metres. With zero point five metre cells, it contains twelve columns and eight rows, for ninety-six cells.
