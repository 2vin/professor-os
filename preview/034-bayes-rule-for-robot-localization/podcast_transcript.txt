Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 34, Bayes Rule for Robot Localization. In the previous class, we introduced localization: estimating where RoboRover is when both motion and sensors are imperfect. A robot usually does not know its exact position. Instead, it maintains a belief, which is a probability distribution over possible positions. Today, we will make one important belief update systematic using Bayes rule. This is the measurement-update step in a localization cycle. The robot predicts where it may have moved, receives a sensor reading, and then updates its belief according to how compatible that reading is with each possible state. Imagine RoboRover somewhere in a four-cell corridor: A, B, C, or D. Before sensing, suppose the belief is evenly distributed: twenty-five percent at each location. Now RoboRover reports, “I detect a blue beacon.” The sensor model tells us how likely that observation is at each location. At A, the probability of detecting blue is ten percent. At B, it is eighty percent. At C, it is twenty percent. At D, it is ten percent. These are likelihoods. They describe the probability of the observation, assuming the robot is at a particular location. They do not need to add up to one across the locations. Bayes rule says: multiply the old belief by the observation’s compatibility, then normalize the results so they add up to one. In ordinary language, each location receives a score. The score is its prior belief multiplied by the likelihood of seeing blue there. Then we divide every score by the total of all scores. Let’s work through the numbers. At A, the score is twenty-five percent times ten percent, or zero point zero two five. At B, it is twenty-five percent times eighty percent, or zero point two zero zero. At C, it is twenty-five percent times twenty percent, or zero point zero five zero. At D, it is again zero point zero two five. The scores total zero point three zero zero. Dividing each score by that total gives the posterior belief: about eight point three percent at A, sixty-six point seven percent at B, sixteen point seven percent at C, and eight point three percent at D. So B is now the strongest candidate, but it is not certain. A noisy sensor has increased the belief in B without eliminating the other possibilities. Pause and predict: with equal prior beliefs, which location should have the highest posterior, and why? Brief beat. The answer is B, because it has the largest likelihood for the blue observation. With equal priors, the largest likelihood produces the largest score. The three key terms are worth keeping distinct. The prior is belief before the observation. The likelihood is the probability of the observation given a state. The posterior is belief after incorporating the observation. This distinction matters because the likelihood, written as the probability of blue given location B, is not the same as the posterior probability of location B given blue. Bayes rule connects those two directions, but they are not interchangeable. A strong prior can also outweigh a favorable likelihood. Suppose the prior is sixty percent at A, ten percent at B, twenty percent at C, and ten percent at D. Using the same blue likelihoods, the scores become zero point zero six zero at A, zero point zero eight zero at B, zero point zero four zero at C, and zero point zero one zero at D. B still has the largest score, but A remains quite competitive because its starting belief was much stronger. This gives us an important interpretation: the posterior depends on both the prior and the evidence. A sensor reading does not act alone. There is also a special case to remember. If every state has the same likelihood for an observation, then that observation provides no information for distinguishing the states. Every score is multiplied by the same factor, and normalization removes that common factor. The belief stays unchanged. For example, if a second sensor reading has likelihood zero point five at every location, it does not change the belief produced by the blue update. A second observation can provide useful information, but there is an important engineering caveat: do not automatically assume that repeated observations are independent. If two readings come from the same blurry image, or from a sensor with a persistent error, they may contain overlapping information. Multiplying their separate likelihoods as though they were independent can make the robot falsely overconfident. If the observations are conditionally independent given the location, then the first posterior can become the prior for the second update. In our example, after blue, a report of “no blue” uses likelihoods of ninety percent at A, twenty percent at B, eighty percent at C, and ninety percent at D. Applying that second update gives approximately eighteen percent at A, thirty-two percent at B, thirty-two percent at C, and eighteen percent at D. B and C tie. Without the independence assumption, the correct quantity is the probability of the second observation given both the location and the first observation. The Python lab implements exactly the same three operations: multiply matching prior and likelihood values, add the scores, and divide each score by the total. You can test it with the equal prior and blue likelihood arrays from our example. The result should sum to one, with its largest value at index one, corresponding to B. The defensive version also checks that the input arrays have matching, nonzero lengths; that values are finite and between zero and one; and that the prior sums to one. It raises an error if every score is zero, because that means the observation is impossible under the supplied model. It also verifies the expected numerical results and confirms that the final beliefs sum to one. That impossible-observation check is another important engineering lesson. If a real sensor produces something your model says cannot happen anywhere, the answer is not to invent a confident location.
