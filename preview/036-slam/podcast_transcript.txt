Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 36, SLAM. RoboRover can now estimate its pose using a particle filter. That answered an important question: where might the robot be? Today we add two harder questions: what does the environment look like, and where am I inside it? SLAM means Simultaneous Localization and Mapping. Localization estimates the robot’s pose: its position and orientation. Mapping estimates the positions or shapes of features in the environment. Simultaneous means these estimates improve together. This is important because pose and map depend on each other. A better pose places new landmarks more accurately. A better map gives the robot stronger references for correcting its pose. Imagine entering a dark warehouse with a measuring tape but no floor plan. You estimate your position by counting steps, but that estimate gradually drifts. Then you see a distinctive blue post and measure its direction and distance. If the post is already on your map, it can help correct your pose. If it is new, you can add it, but its mapped position depends on your current pose estimate. That creates the basic SLAM loop: move and predict a new pose, sense the environment, compare observations with the map, correct the pose, update the map, and repeat. In landmark-based SLAM, a landmark is a recognizable feature, such as a marker, corner, pillar, docking station, or distinctive wall feature. Sensors may report a landmark’s distance and bearing relative to the robot. The robot then transforms that observation into map coordinates. In two dimensions, write the robot pose as x, y, and theta, where x and y are positions in metres and theta is heading in radians. If the landmark observation in the robot’s own coordinate frame is z, the map position is found by rotating that observation by the robot’s heading, then adding the robot’s map position. In plain language: rotate what the robot sees into the map’s orientation, then translate it to where the robot currently believes it is. To predict how a known landmark should appear, reverse that operation. Subtract the robot’s position from the landmark’s map position, then rotate the result by the negative heading. The difference between the predicted observation and the actual sensor measurement is called a measurement residual, or innovation. That disagreement provides evidence for improving the pose, the landmark position, or both. Here is a memorable numerical example. RoboRover estimates that it is at x equals 2 metres, y equals 1 metre, facing 30 degrees. Its sensor sees a landmark at approximately 1.73 metres forward and 1 metre to the right. Rotating that observation by 30 degrees gives approximately 2 metres east and zero metres north. Adding the robot’s position places the landmark at about x equals 4 metres and y equals 1 metre in the map. So RoboRover’s current estimate says the landmark is 4 metres east and 1 metre north of the selected map origin. Now suppose the predicted sensor observation is 1.53 metres forward and 0.90 metres to the right, but the measured observation is 1.73 metres forward and 1 metre to the right. The residual says the landmark is 20 centimetres farther forward and 10 centimetres farther right than predicted. A real estimator would combine that residual with uncertainty rather than blindly accepting one measurement. Pause and predict: if RoboRover sees a landmark for the first time, does it use that landmark to correct its pose, or add it to the map? Brief beat. The answer is: it adds the landmark using its current pose estimate. When it sees the same landmark again, that known feature can provide a reference for correcting the pose. This brings us to drift. Wheel odometry can overestimate or underestimate motion because of wheel slip, unequal wheel diameters, or changing traction. Repeated small errors accumulate. Landmarks provide opportunities to reduce that drift. If the robot eventually recognizes landmarks from the beginning of its journey, it has detected a loop closure. A complete SLAM system can use that connection to adjust many earlier poses and make the entire trajectory more consistent. Loop closure is more than simply correcting against a nearby known landmark. The small hallway simulation in this lesson does not perform loop-closure optimization. The Python lab models the idea with a deliberately simple one-dimensional hallway. The simulator has three hidden landmarks, biased odometry, and ideal relative measurements. A new landmark is stored using the current estimated position. A known landmark directly corrects the estimated position. With the baseline settings, the true final position is 12 metres, while the estimate is 12.3 metres, for an absolute error of 0.3 metres. That remaining error is a crucial engineering lesson. The first landmark locations were created using biased odometry, and the algorithm has no external survey reference to correct the global frame. A map can be internally consistent while still being shifted, rotated, or otherwise misaligned with the physical world. This is sometimes described as the unknown-global-origin problem, or gauge freedom. The lab’s direct-snap correction is useful for learning, but it is not production SLAM. It is not probabilistic fusion, it does not maintain uncertainty, and it does not jointly optimize all poses and landmarks. Real systems may use Kalman filters, particle methods, factor graphs, or other optimization approaches. They must also handle noisy sensors, moving landmarks, missed detections, changing environments, and data-association errors, where one feature is mistakenly matched to another. It is also useful to distinguish map types. A landmark map stores discrete features such as marker centres, corners, or pillar locations. An occupancy grid divides space into cells and estimates whether each cell is free, occupied, or unknown. A landmark map can be compact when features are reliable. An occupancy grid represents obstacles and surfaces more continuously, often with greater memory requirements. Real robots may use both. The practical takeaway is this: SLAM is a coupled estimation problem. Motion prediction carries the robot forward but drifts. Observations provide references, but their map positions depend on the pose estimate.
