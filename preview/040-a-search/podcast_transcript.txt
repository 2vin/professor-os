Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 40, A-star Search. RoboRover already knows how to find a shortest route through a grid using Dijkstra’s algorithm. Dijkstra is reliable because it explores locations in order of known travel cost from the start. But Dijkstra has one limitation: it does not know which direction the goal lies in. If RoboRover is in a large warehouse and the destination is far to the east, Dijkstra may explore many equally expensive locations to the north and south before reaching it. A-star adds a useful question: based on what I know so far, which location seems most promising? That extra guidance comes from a heuristic. A heuristic is an estimate of the remaining cost to the goal. A-star combines the known cost with that estimate. In plain language, Dijkstra chooses the location with the smallest cost already spent. A-star chooses the location with the smallest known cost so far plus an estimate of the cost still remaining. The formula is: f of n equals g of n plus h of n. Here, g of n is the known cost from the start to location n. H of n is the estimated cost from location n to the goal. And f of n is the estimated total route cost through that location. A-star expands the location with the smallest f value. For a grid where RoboRover can move only north, south, east, or west, a natural heuristic is Manhattan distance. It adds the horizontal difference between the current location and the goal to the vertical difference. It is called Manhattan distance because it resembles traveling along city blocks instead of cutting diagonally through buildings. Suppose the goal is five horizontal-or-vertical cells away, and each cell is half a meter wide. The Manhattan estimate is five times half a meter, or two and a half meters. Now suppose RoboRover has already traveled six cells to reach the current location. That known cost is six times half a meter, or three meters. The A-star score is therefore three meters plus two and a half meters, which equals five and a half meters. That five-and-a-half-meter value is an estimate of the total route cost. It is not necessarily the final route length. Shelves or other obstacles may force a detour, making the actual route longer. Here is a useful comparison. Imagine two frontier cells in a warehouse. Cell A is four cells from the start, which is two meters. Its Manhattan estimate to the goal is eight cells, or four meters. Its A-star score is six meters. Cell B is five cells from the start, or two and a half meters. But it is only three cells from the goal, or one and a half meters. Its A-star score is four meters. Dijkstra prefers A because A has the smaller known cost so far. A-star prefers B because B has the smaller estimated total cost. B is slightly farther from RoboRover now, but it points much more directly toward the destination. Pause and predict: why doesn’t A-star simply choose the cell with the smallest heuristic estimate? Take a moment. The answer is that choosing only the smallest heuristic would ignore the cost already spent. That strategy is greedy search, not A-star. A-star balances the journey already taken with the journey still expected. The most important engineering caveat concerns the heuristic. To guarantee a shortest path, the heuristic must be admissible. That means it must never overestimate the true cheapest remaining cost. Manhattan distance is admissible for four-direction movement on a grid with equal movement costs. Every horizontal or vertical difference requires at least that many moves. Obstacles can add movement, but they cannot make the route shorter than that coordinate difference. However, the movement rules and the heuristic must agree. If diagonal movement is allowed, if terrain has different costs, or if turning has a cost, ordinary Manhattan distance may no longer be the right estimate. An overconfident heuristic can cause A-star to ignore a route that is actually shorter, so optimality is no longer guaranteed. This is also why A-star is not a steering command. It plans a route through map locations. A separate motion controller must handle wheel speeds, turning, localization, stopping distance, wheel slip, and real obstacles. In the Python lab, you will compare Dijkstra and A-star on a small warehouse map. The program represents open floor, shelves, a start, a goal, and the selected path. It stores the best known cost, calculates Manhattan distance, and uses a priority queue to select the next location. Conceptually, the lab lets you change the heuristic weight. With a weight of zero, the search behaves like Dijkstra because it uses only the known cost. With a weight of one, it uses the ordinary admissible A-star calculation. With a weight of two, the goal estimate counts more strongly. That may reduce the number of locations inspected, but the usual guarantee of an optimal path is no longer assured. The lab also checks that Dijkstra and ordinary A-star find paths with the same movement cost on the example map. A-star may inspect fewer cells, although the exact difference depends on the map and how ties are handled. Fewer expanded cells are not automatically better; they represent a trade-off between computation time and guarantees. In a real warehouse, the map may be wrong or become outdated. A pallet may move, the map may be shifted, wheel slip may move the rover away from its planned cell, or a sensor may detect a person or cart that was not represented. A practical system builds or receives a map, plans with A-star, follows the route using motion control, checks whether the world still matches the map, and replans when necessary. The practical takeaway is this: A-star is Dijkstra with informed guidance. It uses known travel cost plus a safe estimate of remaining cost to focus the search toward the goal, often while preserving the shortest-path guarantee. Remember the essentials.
