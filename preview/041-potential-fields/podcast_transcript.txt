Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 41, Potential Fields. RoboRover has just learned A* Search, which explores a grid of possible locations and chooses a low-cost route to a goal. Today’s method works differently. Instead of searching through map cells, RoboRover imagines that the goal pulls it forward while obstacles push it away. At each moment, it measures these local influences, combines them, and moves a short distance in the resulting direction. A* chooses a route through discrete map locations. Potential fields create a continuous, local steering direction. A* can plan around difficult layouts, but it may require a map and significant computation. Potential fields can react smoothly to nearby obstacles, but they can also become trapped or behave poorly in awkward geometries. The central idea begins with a scalar quantity called potential. Think of potential as a landscape. The goal is a valley, and obstacles are hills. RoboRover tries to move downhill. The attractive potential increases with squared distance from the goal. Its associated force points toward the goal. In plain language, the farther RoboRover is from its destination, the stronger the goal’s pull in this teaching model. Repulsive potential is based on clearance from an obstacle’s boundary, not merely distance from its center. For a circular obstacle, calculate the distance to its center, then subtract the obstacle radius. That difference is the boundary clearance. Repulsion is active only within an influence distance. As clearance approaches zero, the repulsive influence becomes very strong. At or inside the obstacle, the simulation reports a collision instead of evaluating the singular expression. The total scalar potential is the attraction plus the repulsion from every obstacle. The total steering force is the negative gradient of that potential. That means the force points in the direction where the combined landscape decreases most rapidly. Although we use force-like language, this is often an engineered mathematical tool, not a claim that the robot is literally inside a physical force field. Here is a memorable numerical example. Suppose RoboRover is at position one, zero meters, and the goal is at four, zero meters. With an attraction constant of one newton per meter, the attractive force is three, zero newtons, pointing right. Now place a circular obstacle at two, zero meters with radius zero point two five meters. RoboRover is one meter from the obstacle center, so its clearance from the obstacle boundary is zero point seven five meters. The influence distance is one point five meters, so repulsion is active. Using a repulsion constant of two, the repulsive force is approximately negative two point three seven, zero newtons, pointing left. Add the vectors: three, zero plus negative two point three seven, zero gives approximately zero point six three, zero newtons. RoboRover still moves right, but the obstacle nearly cancels the goal’s pull. A small change in position, parameters, or another nearby obstacle could change the direction. Pause and predict: if the obstacle were farther away than its influence distance, would it change the local steering force? Take a brief beat. The answer is no. Outside the influence distance, that obstacle contributes zero repulsion in this model. In a two-dimensional example, RoboRover may initially head almost directly toward a goal. When a plant container enters the influence distance, repulsion pushes the rover away from the container, and the combined direction bends around it. The repeated process is: sense or estimate the current situation, compute attraction, compute repulsion from nearby obstacles, add the vectors, check the proposed motion for safety, move a limited step, and repeat. The most important engineering caveat is that repulsion is not a guaranteed wall. A large motion step can cross an obstacle even if the endpoint lands outside it. That is why safe simulation checks the entire swept segment from the current position to the candidate position. Endpoint-only checking can miss a collision hidden between two samples. The Python lab demonstrates this process conceptually. It models obstacles as circles, samples the vector field on a grid, draws normalized arrows, and simulates a fixed-step kinematic controller. The net vector is normalized before movement, so the force determines heading while the separate step-size parameter determines how far RoboRover moves per update. This is not a full dynamics simulation with mass, acceleration, velocity, and actuator behavior. The lab also reports why the simulation stopped: perhaps RoboRover reached the goal, collided, encountered a near-zero net force, or exhausted its step limit. It measures minimum swept boundary clearance along both stored positions and connecting segments. That diagnostic is more trustworthy than inferring safety from a plotted curve alone. Potential fields have several important failure modes. A local minimum occurs when the combined force is zero or nearly zero even though the goal has not been reached. Repulsion from multiple obstacles can cancel attraction. The controller may also oscillate from side to side when sensing is noisy or delayed. Strong commands can exceed motor limits, so real systems need saturation: limits on speed, acceleration, and turning rate. Robot shape matters too. A point rover may appear to pass an obstacle while its chassis clips it. A useful first approximation is obstacle inflation: enlarge the modeled obstacle by the rover’s radius and a safety margin. This represents the rover’s center moving around space that the physical body must avoid. It is helpful, but it is not a replacement for verified collision geometry, uncertainty handling, or independent safety systems. The practical takeaway is to treat potential fields as a local steering layer. A global planner such as A* can propose a broad route, while a potential field reacts to newly detected obstacles. In a real robot, combine that steering with reliable sensing, speed limits, clearance checks, emergency stopping, and a strategy for escaping local minima. Let’s recap. A goal creates attraction, obstacles create repulsion, and vector addition produces a local steering direction. Potential is a scalar landscape; the force is the vector derived from its gradient. Boundary clearance is more important than center distance for circular obstacles.
