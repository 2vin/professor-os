Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 42, Robot Arms. Until now, RoboRover has mostly traveled across a surface. In the previous class, Potential Fields, we treated goals and obstacles as influences that could pull or push the rover’s motion. Today, RoboRover gains a new ability: reaching and manipulating objects with an arm. A robot arm is not one solid piece. It is a chain of connected rigid parts that rotate or slide relative to one another. The moving connections are called joints. The rigid parts between joints are called links. And the number of independent ways the mechanism can move is its degrees of freedom, or DOF. A simple arm might be described like this: base, joint, link one, joint, link two, joint, and then the tool. The base provides structural support, but it is not automatically a degree of freedom. We count the independent motions permitted by the joints. A revolute joint rotates around an axis, like a door hinge. A prismatic joint slides along an axis, like a drawer rail. Today, we will mainly study planar revolute arms: arms whose links move on a flat page and whose joints rotate. Imagine a person reaching for a cup. Your upper arm and forearm are fairly rigid links. Your shoulder and elbow are joints. If your elbow could not bend, your hand would be limited to a much smaller range of positions. A two-link planar arm works similarly. Its first joint rotates link one around the base. Its second joint changes the angle between link one and link two. The arm’s current shape is called its configuration, and for this simple arm, the configuration can be represented by two joint angles: theta one and theta two. Under the idealized model of two independently actuated revolute joints moving in a plane, the arm has two degrees of freedom. One independent angle gives one DOF; two independent angles give two DOF. More DOF can make an arm more flexible. It may reach around an obstacle or control the orientation of a tool. But more DOF also means more motors, sensors, software decisions, mass, cost, and possible failure modes. More is not automatically better. Let’s work through one memorable example. RoboRover’s arm has a first link that is zero point three metres long and a second link that is zero point two metres long. The first joint angle is thirty degrees, and the second joint angle is sixty degrees relative to the first link. The elbow position comes from the first link. Its horizontal coordinate is zero point three times the cosine of thirty degrees, which is approximately zero point two six zero metres. Its vertical coordinate is zero point three times the sine of thirty degrees, or approximately zero point one five zero metres. Now consider the second link. Its absolute direction is the first angle plus the second angle: thirty degrees plus sixty degrees, or ninety degrees. So the second link points straight upward. The hand’s horizontal coordinate is approximately zero point two six zero metres, because the second link contributes no horizontal distance when it points straight up. The hand’s vertical coordinate is approximately zero point one five zero plus zero point two zero zero, giving zero point three five zero metres. So the elbow is about twenty-six centimetres right and fifteen centimetres above the base. The hand is about twenty-six centimetres right and thirty-five centimetres above the base. Pause and predict: if the first joint is thirty degrees and the second joint is negative twenty degrees, what is the absolute direction of link two? Brief beat. The answer is ten degrees, because thirty plus negative twenty equals ten. The second angle is relative to link one; it is not automatically an angle measured from the horizontal. That relative-versus-absolute distinction is one of the most important ideas in this lesson. The same arm can form many different configurations. If both links point in the same direction, the total reach is zero point five metres. If the links bend, the hand comes closer to the base. In the ideal unrestricted model, the endpoint position workspace forms an annular region. Its outer radius is the sum of the link lengths, zero point five metres. Its inner radius is the absolute difference, zero point one metre. That statement concerns hand position only. It does not include tool orientation, collisions, or whether a real arm can safely move through every point. Different configurations can sometimes place the hand near the same location. These are often called elbow-up and elbow-down configurations. The most important engineering caveat is that this geometry is an ideal model. It assumes rigid links, exact joint angles, perfect joints, and no flexing. Real arms have joint stops, payload limits, calibration errors, gearbox backlash, and link bending. A heavier object can require more motor torque. A configuration that reaches the target mathematically may collide with a table or exceed a joint limit. Another difficult situation is a singular configuration, such as when the links become collinear while fully extended or fully folded. Near a singularity, small requested hand motions can require very large joint velocities or highly sensitive control commands. A position solution may still exist, but local motion control becomes difficult. The Python lab turns this geometry into a simple simulation. A function called arm points converts the two joint angles from degrees to radians, calculates the base, elbow, and hand coordinates, and then plots several arm configurations. The program checks the worked example against approximate reference values. It also demonstrates why equal scaling on the plot matters: without it, a straight physical link might look bent. You can use the simulation as a joint-angle target game. Choose a target point, guess two joint angles, plot the result, and measure how close the hand comes. This is a basic joint-space experiment: you choose joint coordinates, observe the resulting hand position, and revise your choices.
