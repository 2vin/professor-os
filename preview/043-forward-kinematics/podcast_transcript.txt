Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 43, Forward Kinematics. In the previous class, we gave RoboRover a mechanical arm made from rigid links and revolute joints. Today we answer a precise question: if we know every joint angle and every link length, where is the arm’s end-effector? The end-effector is the tool at the end of the arm, such as a gripper, suction cup, camera, or marker. Calculating its location and orientation from the arm’s geometry is called forward kinematics. Forward kinematics goes from joint angles to pose. Pose means position and orientation together. In the next class, Inverse Kinematics, we will reverse the direction: starting with a desired pose and finding joint angles that could produce it. For today, imagine a two-link planar arm, like two rulers joined at an elbow. Planar means that all motion happens on a flat surface. Link 1 is attached to the rover’s shoulder, Link 2 is attached at the elbow, and the gripper is at the end of Link 2. Joint 1 rotates Link 1 relative to the base. Joint 2 rotates Link 2 relative to Link 1. That last detail is essential: Joint 2 is usually a relative angle, not an angle measured directly from the room. Place the arm’s base at the origin of a coordinate frame. Let the horizontal axis point right and the vertical axis point upward. Let L1 and L2 be the link lengths. Let theta one be Joint 1’s angle measured from the positive horizontal axis. Let theta two be Joint 2’s angle relative to Link 1. The elbow position comes from Link 1. Its horizontal coordinate is L1 times the cosine of theta one, and its vertical coordinate is L1 times the sine of theta one. The gripper receives another displacement from Link 2. But Link 2’s direction in the room is not theta two alone. It is theta one plus theta two, because Link 1 has already rotated the elbow. So the gripper’s horizontal position is L1 times cosine of theta one, plus L2 times the cosine of theta one plus theta two. Its vertical position is L1 times sine of theta one, plus L2 times the sine of theta one plus theta two. The gripper’s orientation, which we can call phi, is also theta one plus theta two. Here is the key distinction. Theta two describes how much Link 2 bends relative to Link 1. Theta one plus theta two describes Link 2’s direction relative to the room. Consider a worked example. Suppose Link 1 is 0.30 metres long and Link 2 is 0.20 metres long. Suppose theta one is 40 degrees and theta two is negative 25 degrees. The negative sign means a clockwise bend, using the convention that counterclockwise angles are positive. The direction of Link 2 is 40 degrees plus negative 25 degrees, which equals 15 degrees. Now resolve both links into horizontal and vertical components. The resulting gripper position is approximately 0.4230 metres to the right of the base and 0.2446 metres above it. The gripper orientation is 15 degrees above horizontal. So the predicted pose is approximately: position, 0.4230 metres right and 0.2446 metres up; orientation, 15 degrees. Notice that the total length of the two links is 0.50 metres, but the gripper is slightly less than 0.50 metres from the base. That makes sense because the links are not perfectly straight. Pause and predict. For this example, will the gripper be above or below the horizontal axis? Will its orientation be 15 degrees, 40 degrees, or negative 25 degrees? And will its distance from the base be greater than, equal to, or less than 0.50 metres? Take a brief moment. The answer is: the gripper is above the horizontal axis, its orientation is 15 degrees, and its distance from the base is less than 0.50 metres. Both links contribute positive vertical components, and the links are not collinear. The trigonometry is simply a way to split each link into horizontal and vertical parts. For a link of length L pointing at angle alpha, the horizontal part is L times cosine of alpha, and the vertical part is L times sine of alpha. There is one important programming detail. Python’s sine and cosine functions expect radians, not degrees. To convert degrees to radians, multiply the degree value by pi divided by 180. This changes the numerical representation, not the physical angle. The Python lab turns this calculation into a reusable forward-kinematics function. Conceptually, the program converts the two input angles to radians, calculates the elbow, calculates Link 2’s room-relative direction, then calculates the gripper position and orientation. It prints the pose and draws the base, elbow, and gripper. Assertions check that the program agrees with the worked example. The drawing also uses equal horizontal and vertical scales, so the geometry is not visually distorted. You can experiment by changing the link lengths and joint angles. For example, setting both joint angles to 45 degrees and zero degrees makes both links point in the same 45-degree direction. Or try theta one equal to 60 degrees and theta two equal to negative 30 degrees. Then Link 1 points at 60 degrees, while Link 2 points at 30 degrees. This is manual search: choose angles, run the forward model, inspect the result, and try again. The most important engineering caveat is that forward kinematics predicts the pose of a model, not automatically the exact pose of real hardware. The equations assume known link lengths, accurate angle measurements, a stationary base, rigid links, and truly planar motion. Real robots can have gearbox backlash, flexible links, encoder offsets, calibration errors, or a slightly moving chassis. Coordinate conventions matter just as much. If one software component treats Joint 2 as a relative angle while another treats it as an absolute angle, the robot may move unexpectedly even when each calculation appears correct. The practical takeaway is this: each link contributes a vector, and forward kinematics adds those vectors. First calculate the elbow.
