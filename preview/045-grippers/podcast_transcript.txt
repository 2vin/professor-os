Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 45: Grippers. RoboRover has already learned inverse kinematics. Given a desired hand position, it can calculate joint angles that place its end-effector there. But reaching an object is not the same as holding it. A gripper must answer three practical questions: where should the fingers touch, how much force should each finger apply, and will the object stay still when the robot lifts or moves it? Today’s central idea is simple: inverse kinematics gets the hand to the object; grasping makes the object stay with the hand. We will focus on force, geometry, and grasping. Force means the pushes and pulls that hold, squeeze, or possibly damage an object. Geometry includes the shape, spacing, and direction of the fingers and the object. Grasping means creating a stable arrangement of contacts so the object can be moved. Imagine holding a smooth book between your hands. Your hands squeeze inward against the book. Those inward forces are called normal forces because they act perpendicular to the book’s surfaces. The book’s weight pulls downward, while friction along the surfaces can act upward to resist sliding. If you squeeze too gently, the book slips. If you squeeze much too hard, you might bend or crush it. So a successful grasp needs enough force to prevent slipping, useful contact locations, appropriate directions, and no more force than the object or gripper can safely tolerate. For one contact, a simplified friction model says that the maximum friction force equals the coefficient of friction multiplied by the normal force. The coefficient of friction is written as mu, and it has no units. A rubber pad may provide more friction than smooth plastic, but the actual value depends on materials, cleanliness, pressure, speed, and surface condition. The object’s weight equals its mass multiplied by gravitational acceleration. Near Earth’s surface, gravitational acceleration is approximately 9.81 meters per second squared. During a stationary lift, the total upward friction must be at least as large as the object’s weight. Engineers usually add a safety factor to account for uncertainty, vibration, imperfect contact, and acceleration. Now consider a two-finger gripper holding an object. Suppose each finger applies the same normal force, N, and both contacts have the same coefficient of friction, mu. The total upward friction capacity is approximately two times mu times N. To avoid slipping during a gentle vertical lift, that friction capacity must be at least the object’s weight. With a safety factor, it must be at least the safety factor multiplied by the weight. Solving for the required normal force per finger gives: the safety factor times mass times gravity, divided by two times the coefficient of friction. Here is a worked example. RoboRover must lift a box with a mass of 0.35 kilograms. The coefficient of friction is 0.60, and the safety factor is 2.0. First, the box’s weight is 0.35 multiplied by 9.81, which equals 3.4335 newtons. Including the safety factor, the required load is 6.867 newtons. Dividing that load between two contacts, and accounting for the coefficient of friction, gives a required normal force of approximately 5.72 newtons per finger. A setting of 6.0 newtons per finger would therefore be slightly above the calculated minimum. Its predicted total friction capacity is 2 multiplied by 0.60 multiplied by 6.0, or 7.2 newtons. That exceeds the safety-adjusted load of 6.867 newtons. Pause and predict: with the same object, friction coefficient, and safety factor, would 5.0 newtons per finger be enough? What about 6.0 newtons? Brief beat. The simplified prediction is no for 5.0 newtons, and yes for 6.0 newtons. The word “predicted” matters. This calculation is a design estimate, not a guarantee. It assumes equal load sharing, stable contact, no object rotation, and a simple friction model. Real surfaces may be dusty, the fingers may not share force equally, and the robot may accelerate while lifting. Geometry is just as important as force. Flat, opposed faces are usually easier to grip than angled or curved surfaces. Centering the object around the gripper and near its center of mass usually reduces unwanted twisting. Larger pads can distribute pressure and reduce local damage. The fingers also need enough clearance to approach without striking nearby objects. A grasp can fail even when the total squeezing force looks sufficient. If one finger touches near the top corner and the other near the bottom corner, the forces may twist the object. If the center of mass is far from the contact region, the object’s weight creates a turning effect, or torque. A simple relationship is torque equals force multiplied by perpendicular distance. We will study that connection more fully in Dynamics. Too much force creates its own problems. It can crush soft packaging, deform a plastic container, damage a sensor, stall or overheat an actuator, or make the object difficult to release. A good gripper needs enough force, not the maximum possible force. Also, a motor command is not the same thing as fingertip force. A command such as sixty percent motor power does not directly reveal the contact force. The result depends on gear ratios, linkage geometry, friction, battery voltage, motor speed, and mechanical stops. A real gripper may need force sensing, current-based estimation, or calibration tests. Compliant rubber pads can help by tolerating small alignment errors and spreading contact, although they can also make finger position less predictable. In the Python lab, the program calculates the required force per finger and visualizes predicted slip and predicted safe regions for different grip forces. It verifies the worked example, tests 5.0 and 6.0 newtons, and plots the threshold where the simplified prediction changes. The graph is visualizing a mathematical model; it is not measuring RoboRover. You can explore the model by changing one parameter at a time. Increasing the object’s mass increases the required force. Improving the friction coefficient reduces the required squeezing force. You can also set a gripper force limit.
