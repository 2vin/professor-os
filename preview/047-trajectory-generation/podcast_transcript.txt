Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 47: Trajectory Generation. RoboRover has just learned about dynamics: how forces and torques create acceleration, and how mass, friction, and motor limits affect motion. Now we turn the question around. If RoboRover needs to move from one place to another, what motion should we command at every instant? “Move one metre forward” is not enough. The robot also needs to know where it should be after half a second, how fast it should be moving, when to speed up, when to slow down, and whether the requested acceleration is physically possible. That is trajectory generation: creating a time-ordered motion plan for position, velocity, and sometimes acceleration. First, distinguish a path from a trajectory. A path describes the geometric route. For example, a straight line from the laboratory door to the workbench. A trajectory adds timing and speed. Traveling that metre in two seconds and traveling it in eight seconds use the same path, but they are different trajectories. Smoothness matters because real robots have limited motor torque, flexible parts, wheel traction limits, backlash in gears and belts, and sensors that respond imperfectly. An instant jump from zero speed to a high speed would require an unrealistically large acceleration. Rapid changes in acceleration also create high jerk, which is the rate of change of acceleration. A gentler motion begins at rest, speeds up gradually, perhaps travels at a useful speed, then slows down gradually and finishes at rest. For a simple one-dimensional trajectory, suppose RoboRover moves from starting position x-zero to final position x-final in total time T. We define normalized time as current time divided by total time. Call this value s. It runs from zero to one and has no units. A useful smooth position profile is the quintic smoothstep. Its progress value is ten s cubed, minus fifteen s to the fourth power, plus six s to the fifth power. The commanded position is the starting position plus the travel distance multiplied by that progress value. At s equals zero, progress is zero. At s equals one, progress is one. This profile also gives zero starting velocity, zero final velocity, zero starting acceleration, and zero final acceleration. “Quintic” simply means that the highest power is five. It does not refer to the number of wheels or motors. A linear profile is simpler, but if the robot begins and ends at rest, its velocity would need to jump instantly at the endpoints. A cubic profile can specify position and velocity at both ends, but its endpoint acceleration generally is not zero. The quintic profile gives us smooth position, velocity, and acceleration for this lesson. The timing has an important physical consequence. Velocity scales with one over T. Acceleration scales with one over T squared. So if the same distance is completed in half the time, the speed demand doubles, but the acceleration demand becomes four times larger. Here is a worked example. RoboRover moves from zero metres to 1.2 metres in four seconds. After one second, normalized time is one quarter. Substituting one quarter into the quintic progress function gives a progress value of about 0.1035. Multiplying by 1.2 metres, the robot is at about 0.124 metres from its start. Its velocity is about 0.316 metres per second, and its acceleration is about 0.422 metres per second squared. So it is moving forward and still speeding up. At the midpoint, after two seconds, the robot is at 0.6 metres. Its acceleration is zero, and its velocity reaches the profile’s maximum of 0.5625 metres per second. Notice that zero acceleration at the midpoint does not mean zero velocity. Also, the peak acceleration occurs before and after the midpoint, with a magnitude of about 0.433 metres per second squared. Pause and predict: if we reduce the total time from four seconds to two seconds, does the final position change, and what happens to the acceleration? …The final position stays 1.2 metres, because the endpoints did not change. The velocity demand doubles, while the acceleration demand becomes four times larger. The robot still begins and ends at rest, but the motion is much more demanding. The Python lab turns this mathematics into a sampled trajectory. It evaluates position, velocity, and acceleration every 0.02 seconds, plots the three curves, and uses assertions to check the endpoints, midpoint values, worked example, and analytical peak acceleration. You do not need to read the program line by line. The important idea is that normalized time creates the shape, while distance divided by time and distance divided by time squared convert that shape into physical velocity and acceleration. The lab also searches candidate durations for a 1.2-metre motion under hypothetical limits of 0.5 metres per second and 0.5 metres per second squared. For this quintic profile, the shortest tested duration satisfying both limits is 4.50 seconds. That search reports a suitable duration; it does not modify the trajectory to enforce limits during physical execution. That is the most important engineering caveat. A mathematically smooth trajectory is only a desired motion. It does not guarantee that the real robot can follow it. Motors may saturate when they reach their maximum available command. Wheel traction may be insufficient. Commands may arrive late, sampling may be irregular, or calibration may be wrong. If the wheel diameter is estimated incorrectly, the commanded distance can differ from the actual distance, even if the plot looks perfect. A real trajectory generator should therefore consider motor torque, velocity, acceleration, jerk, traction, obstacles, timing, and tracking performance. A lower-level controller then uses motors and measurements to reduce the difference between the desired and actual motion. The example today is one-dimensional. A complete planar rover trajectory may also need orientation, heading, curvature, and coordinated left and right wheel speeds. The one-dimensional case is a useful teaching model, not a complete solution for every rover motion.
