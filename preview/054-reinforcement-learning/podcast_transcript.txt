Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 54, Reinforcement Learning. In the previous class, we used Gazebo and physics simulation to study how RoboRover moves in a virtual world. The simulator can model contact, friction, gravity, collisions, sensor readings, and motor motion without putting a real machine at risk. Today we add a new question: how can RoboRover improve its behavior by trying actions and receiving consequences? That is the central question of reinforcement learning, or RL. Reinforcement learning does not begin with a programmer specifying every correct action. Instead, an agent interacts with an environment, receives rewards or penalties, and gradually learns which actions tend to produce better long-term results. The three essential ideas are the agent, the reward, and the policy. The agent is the decision-making participant. For RoboRover, that usually means the software, policy, and stored experience that choose actions. The physical chassis is part of the robot, but in an RL diagram, “agent” usually refers to the decision-making system. The environment is everything outside the agent that responds to its actions. In Gazebo, that can include walls, floor friction, packages, simulated people, sensor noise, battery effects, and the physics engine. A reward is a numerical signal describing how desirable a recent outcome was. A successful delivery might receive plus ten points. A collision might receive minus five. A reward can also be zero or delayed until a task is complete. Rewards are score units chosen by the designer. They are not automatically meters, joules, or newtons, and they do not directly tell the robot how to steer. A policy is the rule used to select an action from the current situation. It might be a table, a flowchart, a Python function, or a neural network. A deterministic policy chooses the same action for the same state. A stochastic policy chooses among actions with specified probabilities. Here is the interaction loop. The agent receives an observation, chooses an action, and the environment changes. The environment then provides a reward and a new situation. The agent uses this experience to adjust future choices. An observation is information available to the agent, such as camera pixels, range measurements, or encoder readings. A state is an abstract description of the relevant situation used for decision-making. A state may be directly available in a simple simulation, or estimated from partial and noisy observations. In many robotics systems, the agent cannot observe the complete physical state. Imagine RoboRover in a warehouse with a package. It can choose a wide route that is slow but reliable, or a shortcut that is fast when clear but sometimes blocked by a fallen box. The warehouse is the environment. RoboRover’s decision-making software is the agent. The route-selection rule is the policy. The numerical result after each attempt is the reward. The robot is not merely reacting to one sensor reading. It is trying to discover behavior that produces good results over many interactions. Now, an important distinction: the Python activity in this lesson is a two-armed bandit experiment, not a complete sequential reinforcement-learning system. In a one-state bandit problem, there is one decision situation, several actions called arms, and one reward after each selected action. There is no modeled state transition or multi-step consequence. In this example, choosing a route does not alter a modeled warehouse state. Each route has a stationary reward distribution, and every selection produces an independent sample from that distribution. Sequential reinforcement learning is different. An action can change the next state, and later rewards can depend on the sequence of earlier actions. Moving RoboRover into one grid cell may change which cells are reachable next and affect future rewards. Q-learning, which we will study next class, addresses that state-transition problem. Let’s look at returns. Suppose RoboRover receives four points for finding the correct aisle, loses one point because of a delay, and then receives six points for delivering the package. The undiscounted total is four plus negative one plus six, which equals nine points. Sometimes future rewards receive less weight. With a discount factor of zero point nine, the calculation is four, plus zero point nine times negative one, plus zero point nine squared times six. That gives seven point ninety-six points. The robot still values the later delivery reward, but treats it as slightly less important than an equally large immediate reward. This discount factor is a modeling choice. It does not mean the package physically loses ten percent of its value every second. Here is our worked robotics example. The wide route succeeds with probability zero point ninety-five, earning four points when successful and losing one point when blocked. The shortcut succeeds with probability zero point seventy, earning eight points when successful and losing ten points when blocked. For the wide route, expected reward is zero point ninety-five times four, plus zero point zero five times negative one. That equals three point seventy-five points. For the shortcut, it is zero point seventy times eight, plus zero point thirty times negative ten. That equals two point six points. So the shortcut has the larger success reward, but the wide route has the better expected reward. Fastest when successful is not automatically best overall. Pause and predict: after one wide-route result of four points and one shortcut result of eight points, which route will a greedy policy choose next? Brief beat. It chooses the shortcut, because its observed average is eight while the wide route’s observed average is four. If the shortcut then produces negative ten, its average becomes negative one, while the wide route remains at four. The greedy policy then chooses the wide route. This shows why early luck can influence later choices. It also introduces exploration and exploitation. Exploration means trying actions to gain information. Exploitation means choosing the action that currently appears best. The Python lab models this idea conceptually. It tests each route early, samples rewards from the route’s fixed success distribution, stores counts and total rewards, and calculates a sample-average reward for each action.
