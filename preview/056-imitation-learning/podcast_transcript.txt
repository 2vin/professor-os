Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 56, Imitation Learning. In the previous class, RoboRover used Q-learning. It tried actions, received rewards from the environment, and updated action values. Today, RoboRover learns in a different way: by watching an expert. This is imitation learning, also called learning from demonstrations. Instead of discovering every useful action through trial and error, the robot receives examples such as, “When the robot is in this situation, the expert chose this action.” The central idea is simple: demonstrations turn expert decisions into a policy. A policy is a rule for choosing an action from a situation, or observation. A demonstration usually contains an observation and the action chosen at that moment. For instance, the robot might observe its position, camera image, wheel speeds, or distance readings. The expert’s action might be move upward, turn right, steer left, or change the drone’s thrust. A direct form of imitation learning is called behavior cloning. Behavior cloning treats each demonstration step like a labeled example. The observation is the input, and the expert’s action is the label. After seeing enough examples, the robot learns an estimated policy that maps a new observation to a predicted action. For a small, discrete problem, this can be as simple as counting. Suppose the same observation appears four times in the demonstrations. The expert chose RIGHT three times and UP once. The learned policy chooses RIGHT because it was the most common action. In mathematical language, it chooses the action with the largest demonstration count. That is what “argmax” means: choose the action with the greatest value. Here is a memorable example. RoboRover must travel around a blocked central wall to reach a charging beacon on the right. An expert drives around the upper side: up, up, then right several times, and finally down to the beacon. RoboRover records the situation and action at every step. Later, when it encounters one of those recorded situations, it repeats the demonstrated action. The learner is not calculating a new route. It is copying the actions associated with the observations it has seen. Now imagine two demonstrations. One begins from the middle-left and travels around the top of the wall. Another begins lower down and travels around the bottom. The learning process records each visited tile, records the expert’s next movement, and counts the actions associated with each tile. During operation, RoboRover looks up its current tile and repeats the learned action. This makes the math concrete. If the same observation is paired with RIGHT three times and UP once, the probability-like fraction for RIGHT is three out of four, or 75 percent. But that percentage is only a description of the demonstrations. It is not a guarantee that RIGHT is correct. Pause and predict: what should RoboRover do at the top starting position if the demonstration begins by moving UP? And what should happen at a position that never appeared in either demonstration? Brief beat. The answers are UP for the top starting position, and no learned action for the unseen position. Imitation alone does not guarantee a correct answer there. That limitation is the most important engineering caveat in this lesson. A robot may fail when it encounters a situation missing from its demonstrations. This problem is called distribution shift: the situations encountered during operation differ from the situations present in training. There is also compounding error. Imagine an expert driving perfectly down the center of a narrow hallway. The training data mostly contains centered observations. During deployment, the robot drifts slightly left. It now sees an observation that may be rare or absent from training. Its next action may be poor, causing it to drift farther. That creates an even more unfamiliar observation, and the errors can build on one another. This is why copying expert behavior is not the same as copying the expert’s goals, understanding, or safety judgment. A demonstration shows what the expert did, not necessarily why the expert did it. The Python lab models this idea with a small grid. It includes two demonstrated routes, a function that converts adjacent positions into action labels, and a learning step that counts actions for each observation. The resulting policy replays the routes and checks that they reach the goal. It also deliberately checks an unseen position and shows that the policy has no action there. The simulation gives RoboRover its exact tile position, almost like a perfect position sensor. A real robot usually has to estimate its position from noisy sensors. Real systems also face camera changes, motor delay, different speeds, battery effects, wheel friction, and inconsistent human actions. Timing matters too. Suppose one forward command lasts one quarter of a second, and RoboRover travels at zero point two meters per second. The distance for one command is speed times time: zero point two times zero point two five, which equals zero point zero five meters. Three commands cover zero point one five meters. The label FORWARD was copied from the expert, but the actual distance depends on RoboRover’s hardware and timing. An expert’s command might move five centimeters, while another robot moves seven. That leads to the practical takeaway: imitation learning can provide useful behavior quickly, especially when exploration is slow or mistakes are expensive. But a learned action should not automatically be trusted. A practical system can place safety checks after the learned policy. For example, the policy may suggest FORWARD, while a distance sensor overrides that command if an obstacle is dangerously close. If an observation is unseen, a safer fallback might be to stop and ask for help rather than guess. This also explains the difference from Q-learning. Q-learning improves action values through trial, environmental feedback, and rewards. Imitation learning starts with examples supplied by an expert. It can learn a useful route immediately, but it does not automatically search for a better route or recover from situations absent from the demonstrations.
