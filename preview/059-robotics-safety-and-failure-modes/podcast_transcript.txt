Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 59, Robotics Safety and Failure Modes. RoboRover already has a navigation stack: sensors produce measurements, measurements support a state estimate, the planner chooses a motion command, and motor controllers drive the wheels. But useful motion is not automatically safe motion. A planner might request too much speed for a narrow corridor. A sensor might report an impossible value. Or a communication failure might leave a motor controller holding its last command. Today, we add an introductory safety-monitoring layer around normal robot behavior. The central idea is simple: even if navigation requests an action, a separate safety layer asks whether that action is safe to allow. It can permit the command, reduce it, reject it, slow the robot, stop it, or report a fault. We will focus on three tools: limits, validation, and redundancy. A normal operating limit keeps routine behavior inside the robot’s intended envelope. For example, RoboRover might normally be limited to zero point six meters per second. A protective limit is part of a defined response to a hazard, such as stopping when a validated obstacle is too close. Validation asks whether a measurement or command is believable and usable. Is it finite? Is it recent? Is it within range? Does it change plausibly? Does it agree with another measurement? These ideas use thresholds, but they answer different engineering questions. A software threshold is not automatically a validated protective function. Consider RoboRover carrying a hot cup through a laboratory. Its navigation software requests zero point eight meters per second. That might be fine on an empty test track, but not near a person, a wall, or a slippery surface. A safety monitor could reduce the speed to zero point six meters per second, reject a distance reading of eighty meters if the sensor’s useful range is five meters, and respond cautiously if two distance sensors disagree. Validation is especially important because a reading can look reasonable and still be unusable. A distance of one point two meters may be numerically valid but three seconds old. For a moving robot, stale information can be dangerous. A failed check should not lead us to invent a replacement value and treat it as certain. Instead, the robot may slow down, stop, or enter a defined fallback mode. Redundancy provides additional evidence. Two distance sensors, or wheel speed combined with an independent motion sensor, can help reveal a failure. But redundancy is strongest when the failure modes are different. Sensors mounted together may both fail because of dust, glare, fog, shared power, calibration, or a common software defect. That is called a common-cause failure. Two sensors in a teaching simulation do not automatically create a safety-rated architecture. Now let’s work through stopping distance. A moving robot needs distance to react and brake. The travel distance after detection equals speed multiplied by reaction time, plus speed squared divided by twice the braking deceleration. Reaction time includes processing, communication, and actuator response after the safety decision has the needed information. Sensor latency must be included somewhere, but not counted twice. Suppose RoboRover travels at zero point eight meters per second. Reaction time is zero point one five seconds, and braking deceleration is one point two meters per second squared. During reaction, it travels zero point twelve meters. Braking distance is approximately zero point two seven meters. So travel after detection is about zero point thirty-nine meters. Now add zero point fifteen meters for uncertainty and zero point ten meters for required clearance. If the sensor is zero point ten meters ahead of the nearest hazardous robot surface, subtract that offset. The required sensor distance is approximately zero point fifty-four meters. Pause and predict: if RoboRover’s speed doubles while braking performance stays the same, what happens to braking distance? Brief beat. It becomes four times larger, because braking distance depends on speed squared. This is why speed limits can strongly affect safety margins. These calculations are estimates, not universal safety limits. Real designs must measure braking, latency, uncertainty, geometry, surface effects, and payload behavior. This is the most important engineering caveat in the lesson: a software monitor, duplicated sensor, or computed threshold does not by itself prove that the robot is safe. Real systems require an operating context, hazard analysis, validated protective measures, testing, and appropriate engineering standards. Also, stopping is not always safest. A sudden stop could drop a load, block an escape route, or allow a robot on a slope to roll. The response must fit the robot, environment, stored energy, and hazard. In the Python lab, you will simulate a safety monitor conceptually rather than read code line by line. The monitor limits speed to zero point six meters per second, rejects stale, invalid, or out-of-range sensor data, checks sensor disagreement before averaging readings, rejects negative commands and non-finite values such as NaN or infinity, and latches a stop after a serious fault. The example has eight time steps, each lasting zero point one seconds. At the first two steps, commands of zero point four and zero point five meters per second are accepted. At the third step, a command of zero point seven is clipped to zero point six and reported as limited. At the fourth step, the sensors report one point two meters and zero point seven nine meters. Their difference is zero point four one meters, exceeding the disagreement limit of zero point fifteen. The monitor stops the robot before averaging those conflicting readings, and the stop remains latched for the rest of the simulation. The idealized commanded-distance total is zero point fifteen meters. But that is not measured physical travel. It simply adds allowed speed multiplied by the time step. It does not model acceleration, braking, actuator response, wheel slip, or the stopping-distance equation. A latched stop is useful because the robot does not automatically resume after an unexplained fault.
