Welcome to Professor OS. This is Class 60, “Capstone: Design an Autonomous Robot.” Today we bring the robotics journey together. RoboRover has learned to sense, move, make decisions, and respond to failures. Now we will use an engineering process to design RoboRover Scout, a small wheeled robot that travels at a fixed commanded speed along a marked, mostly straight indoor lane. The mission is simple but precise. RoboRover begins autonomous operation when an operator starts the mission. After that initiation, the robot selects its own actions. It carries a lightweight sample container, travels toward a collection zone, stops before obstacles, and reports its mission state. Teleoperated driving does not count as autonomous operation, and advanced route planning is outside today’s scope. The central idea is this chain: mission goal, requirements, architecture, implementation, tests and evidence, then revision. A mission goal such as “carry a sample safely” is not specific enough to build or test. We translate it into measurable requirements. For example, RoboRover shall travel at least 2.0 meters along the marked lane. It shall stop with at least 0.30 meters of final physical clearance from a detected obstacle. It shall report its mission status. It shall enter a safe state if obstacle sensing becomes unavailable. It shall retain at least 20 percent of its starting usable energy after the mission. It shall remain within 0.15 meters of the lane centerline, retain the sample container, deliver it fully inside the collection zone, and stop with a TIMEOUT status if the mission is incomplete after 120 active control steps. Notice that a requirement says what must be true. It does not prescribe the solution. “Use a forward distance sensor and a safety supervisor” is a design choice, not a requirement. A measured clearance of 0.34 meters is a measured result, not a requirement. Keeping these categories separate prevents an estimate from being mistaken for evidence. Architecture assigns responsibilities. The motion controller manages speed and lane position. The mission manager tracks pickup, travel, delivery, completion, and status messages. A safety supervisor evaluates obstacles, sensor health, battery reserve, and timeout. Sensors provide information, motors provide actuation, and the power subsystem distributes energy. An independent emergency-stop or power-cutoff path should be able to inhibit motor power without depending only on ordinary mission software. The state machine makes behavior explicit. During RUNNING, RoboRover drives, senses, logs, and updates progress. It may reach MISSION_COMPLETE, or it may enter SAFE_STOP_OBSTACLE, SAFE_STOP_SENSOR_FAILURE, SAFE_STOP_LOW_BATTERY, or TIMEOUT. Each state has a defined reason and a defined response. A failure must not quietly become a success. Here is a worked example involving obstacle stopping. Suppose the robot travels at 0.20 meters per second and checks its sensor every 0.10 seconds. Assume a reaction distance of 0.020 meters, a measured braking distance of 0.08 meters, a required final physical clearance of 0.30 meters, and a provisional uncertainty allowance of 0.10 meters. For this preliminary illustration, assume the sensor and frontmost point have zero offset. The stopping threshold is the required clearance, plus the sensor-to-frontmost-point offset, plus reaction distance, braking distance, and uncertainty allowance. That gives 0.30 plus zero, plus 0.020, plus 0.080, plus 0.10: a total of 0.50 meters. But this is only an illustrative design estimate. It is not a universal hardware setting and not a validated safety distance. If the sensor is actually 0.05 meters behind the frontmost point, the corresponding preliminary threshold becomes 0.55 meters. Real validation must measure sensor bias, mounting geometry, latency, braking behavior, speed variation, and uncertainty. Pause and predict: if an obstacle is at 1.5 meters in the simplified Python model, and the sensor range is 0.4 meters, where does the modeled reference point stop? Brief beat. It stops at 1.1 meters, because the obstacle position is 1.5 and the modeled stopping range is 0.4. That is reference-point clearance, not physical front-bumper clearance. The Python lab models selected mission logic. It checks idealized travel, obstacle decisions, sensor-failure behavior, energy-reserve logic, status reporting, timeout behavior, and input validation. It uses a control interval of 0.10 seconds and requires that one movement step not exceed the sensor range. The model does not represent wheel slip, sensor noise, braking dynamics, lane deviation, sample retention, full-containment delivery, or physical mounting offsets. Passing its assertions verifies only the behaviors the model actually includes. Energy provides another useful example. With 9 watts of drive power and 2 watts for the computer and sensors, total modeled power is 11 watts. Over 80 seconds, energy use is 880 joules. If the reserve must be 20 percent, the starting usable energy must be at least 880 divided by 0.80, or 1,100 joules. With a modeled starting energy of 1,200 joules, 320 joules remain. That is about 26.7 percent, so the idealized model passes the reserve requirement. Before each movement step, the controller should predict whether the step would reduce remaining energy below the reserve. Equality is allowed because the requirement says “at least 20 percent.” Testing produces evidence, not confidence alone. Each test needs a setup, an input or condition, an expected result, a measurement, and a pass rule. For obstacle clearance, measure from the robot’s actual frontmost physical point to a defined obstacle reference line. For lane position, checkpoint readings alone cannot prove the maximum deviation; use continuous tracking or define a sampling and interpolation method that supports the maximum claim. For sample delivery, the entire 0.20 by 0.15 meter container footprint must be strictly inside the 0.40 by 0.35 meter collection zone. The container center alone is not enough. Verification asks whether the implementation satisfies its requirements. Validation asks whether the completed robot meets the mission need in its intended environment. Simulation can support logic verification, but physical trials are required for claims about actual clearance, lane deviation, sample retention and delivery, actuator stopping, and measured energy behavior. Your practical takeaway is to build a traceability matrix. Connect every requirement to its responsible subsystem, its test, its evidence, and its pass rule.
